Feynman and Quantum Electrodynamics

A theory tested to twelve digits

1948 · Richard Feynman

Quantum electrodynamics, QED, is the theory of light and electrons. In his 1985 book of lectures for a general audience, Richard Feynman explained it with little arrows: every way an event can happen contributes an arrow, and the arrows add[2]. His 1949 paper had turned the same idea into a practical method of calculation[1]; the sum-over-paths idea itself is in his 1948 paper[9].

The simulation adds the arrows for light bouncing off a mirror and a thin layer of glass, then compares QED's prediction for the electron's magnetism with the measured value.

Press play: one arrow per strip of mirror is added.

One Arrow per Path

In QED the chance of an event is found by adding up amplitudes, one for each way the event can happen. Feynman drew each amplitude as an arrow[2]. For light, the arrow turns like the hand of a very fast stopwatch while the light travels, once per wavelength of path, so its direction is set by the path length $l$:

$$\text{arrow} \propto e^{\,2\pi i\,l/\lambda}$$

Add the arrows head to tail. The probability is the square of the length of the final arrow[2].

Why a Mirror Reflects at Equal Angles

Light from S can reach P by bouncing off any part of the mirror. Near the point where the angles are equal, the path length barely changes from one strip to the next, so those arrows point almost the same way and add up. Far from it the length changes quickly, the arrows spin round and cancel. The light seems to follow the shortest path because that is where the arrows agree. Remove the middle strips and the reflection drops to about a tenth, even though most of the mirror is still there.

Slide the detector and the strips whose arrows agree move with it: the bright part of the mirror is always where the angles are equal. Now take the end strips, whose arrows spin round and cancel, and scrape off every strip whose arrow points backwards. The rest add up, and light reaches P from a part of the mirror that sent almost nothing before. This is a diffraction grating[2].

Why Thin Layers Are Coloured

Glass reflects some light from its front surface and some from its back. At normal incidence each surface reflects an amplitude $r = (n-1)/(n+1) = 0.2$ for $n = 1.5$, which is 4 % of the light[8]. Feynman's rule is two arrows of length 0.2: the front one reversed, the back one turned by the extra path $2nd$ through the layer[2]. Depending on the thickness and the colour of the light they cancel or add, so the reflection runs from 0 to 16 %. Different wavelengths peak at different thicknesses, which is why thin layers look coloured.

The Electron's Magnetism, Digit by Digit

Dirac's equation predicts that the electron's magnetic moment corresponds to a g-factor of exactly 2. Measurements by Polykarp Kusch and Henry Foley, published in 1948, found it slightly larger[4]. The same year Julian Schwinger calculated QED's first correction, $a_e = (g-2)/2 = \alpha/2\pi$[3]. With today's fine-structure constant, $\alpha^{-1} = 137.035\,999\,177$[7], that is 0.001 161 4.

The calculation has since been carried to the fifth power of $\alpha$. In 2018 Tatsumi Aoyama, Toichiro Kinoshita and Makiko Nio gave $a_e = 1\,159\,652\,182.032(720) \times 10^{-12}$[5], using the fine-structure constant of 2011; with later values of $\alpha$ the prediction moves by about 1 part in $10^{12}$, which is the limit the authors mention. In 2023 a measurement gave $g/2 = 1.001\,159\,652\,180\,59(13)$, to 0.13 parts per trillion[6]. Theory and measurement agree to about one part in $10^{12}$; the authors note that the comparison is now limited by disagreeing measurements of $\alpha$, on which the prediction depends[6].

What the Simulation Assumes

The mirror arrows all have the same length; real amplitudes also depend on distance and angle. The wavelength is drawn far larger than real light compared with the mirror so the turning can be seen. The glass layer uses the two-arrow rule and ignores multiple reflections inside the layer, and the colour swatch is an approximation from three wavelengths. The g-factor digits are quoted from the sources, except Schwinger's term, which is calculated.

Key Takeaway
  • QED adds one amplitude arrow per possible path; the probability is the square of the final arrow's length.
  • A mirror reflects at equal angles because only there do neighbouring paths have nearly equal lengths and their arrows agree; a thin layer's two arrows explain its colours.
  • QED's prediction of the electron's magnetic moment matches the 2023 measurement to about 1 part in $10^{12}$.
