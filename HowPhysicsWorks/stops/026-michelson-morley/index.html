The Michelson-Morley Experiment

The most famous null result in physics

1887 · Michelson & Morley

The question

How far should the fringes have moved when the apparatus turned, if the ether stood still, and how does that compare with what was seen?

In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley, working in Cleveland, Ohio, tried to detect Earth's motion through the luminiferous ether, the medium that light waves were then thought to travel in[1]. If the ether stood still while Earth moved through it, turning their apparatus by 90° should have moved its interference fringes by about 0.4 of a fringe. They saw less than a twentieth of that[1].

Light in a Moving Medium

In the nineteenth century light was understood as a wave, and a wave was thought to need something to wave in. That medium was called the luminiferous ether. If light moves at speed $c$ relative to the ether, an apparatus moving through the ether at speed $v$ should see light take slightly different times to cross it in different directions.

Earth orbits the Sun at about 29.8 km/s[2], so $v/c$ is about $10^{-4}$. A round trip of length $2L$ along the motion takes $\frac{2L/c}{1 - v^2/c^2}$; the same trip across the motion takes $\frac{2L/c}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$. To second order in $v/c$ the trip into the wind takes longer by $L v^2/c^3$; in path length that is $L v^2/c^2$, about one part in $10^8$[9]. That is far too small to time directly, but an interferometer compares the two paths against the wavelength of the light.

The Fringe Shift

An interferometer splits a beam in two, sends the halves along perpendicular arms, and recombines them. Where the waves arrive in step the light is bright; where they arrive out of step it is dark. The result is a row of bright and dark bands, the fringes. A change in the path difference of one wavelength $\lambda$ moves the fringes by one band.

Turning the apparatus by 90° swaps the roles of the two arms, so the path difference changes by twice $L v^2/c^2$. The predicted shift, in fringes, is

$$\Delta N_{90^\circ} = \frac{2 L v^2}{\lambda c^2}$$

and at any angle $\theta$ between one arm and the motion the fringe position follows

$$N(\theta) = \frac{\Delta N_{90^\circ}}{2}\cos 2\theta$$

Michelson and Morley's mirrors folded the light back and forth so that each arm's path was about 11 m, about $2 \times 10^7$ wavelengths of yellow light. With Earth's orbital speed they expected a shift of about 0.4 of a fringe[1]. The simulation starts with those values: 11 m, 30 km/s and 550 nm give 0.40.

The 1881 Attempt and the 1887 Apparatus

Michelson first tried the experiment in 1881 at Potsdam, with arms about 1.2 m long[3]; the formula above gives an expected shift of only about 0.04 fringe for that apparatus. That was small compared with his errors, and his analysis had left out the effect of the motion on the arm across it, which halves the prediction[1][4]. For the 1887 repeat with Morley the optics were mounted on a stone slab about 1.5 m square, floating on mercury so it could be turned slowly and continuously, one turn in six minutes[1]. They read the fringe position at sixteen directions as the stone turned, at noon and in the evening, on several days in July 1887[1].

They reported that the displacement was "certainly less than the twentieth part" of the expected 0.4, and probably less than the fortieth[1]: less than 0.02 of a fringe. In the simulation the lower fringe strip stands for that observation, and the graph draws the bound as a band, because what they reported is an upper limit, not a curve.

What They Concluded, and What Came After

Michelson and Morley did not conclude that the ether does not exist. They concluded that the relative motion of Earth and the ether, if any, must be small, small enough to rule out Fresnel's explanation of stellar aberration, which assumed an ether at rest[1]. One possibility then on the table was that Earth drags the nearby ether along with it.

Toggle the contraction in the simulation and the two light pulses return together at every angle; stops 029 and 030 come back to this. In 1889 George FitzGerald[5] and in 1892 Hendrik Lorentz[6] independently proposed that objects moving through the ether shrink along the direction of motion by the factor $\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$, just enough to cancel the expected shift. In 1905 Einstein's special relativity made the speed of light the same for every observer moving at constant velocity, and the ether was no longer needed[7]. His paper mentions "unsuccessful attempts to discover any motion of the earth relatively to the light medium" without naming this experiment; how much it influenced him is still discussed by historians.

Modern versions use lasers and rotating optical cavities. One reported in 2009 found no direction dependence of the speed of light at the level of about one part in $10^{17}$[8].

What the Simulation Leaves Out

The prediction uses the textbook second-order formula for an ether at rest relative to the Sun, and the speed slider stands for the whole speed through the ether. A real analysis would add the Sun's own motion and the daily and yearly changes in direction. The fringes are drawn as an ideal pattern; the real ones were viewed through a telescope and read by eye against a fiducial mark. The shifts are computed exactly from the two round-trip times, $t(\theta) = \frac{2L}{c}\frac{\sqrt{1-\beta^2\sin^2\theta}}{1-\beta^2}$ with $\beta = v/c$, not taken from data; at $\beta = 10^{-4}$ this agrees with the formula above. The light pulses in the top view are drawn at an exaggerated speed (shown on screen) so the lag can be seen.

Key Takeaway
  • A stationary ether predicts that turning the interferometer by 90° moves the fringes by $2Lv^2/(\lambda c^2)$: about 0.4 of a fringe for the 1887 apparatus (cited).
  • Michelson and Morley reported less than 0.02, probably less than 0.01 (cited). They concluded that Earth's motion relative to the ether is small, not that the ether does not exist.
  • The length contraction of FitzGerald and Lorentz explained the null result within the ether picture; special relativity in 1905 removed the need for an ether.
