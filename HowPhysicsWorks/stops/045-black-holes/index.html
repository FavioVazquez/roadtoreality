Black Holes

Gravity so strong not even light can escape

Predicted 1916, imaged 2019 · Schwarzschild & the EHT

A few weeks after Einstein published the field equations of general relativity, Karl Schwarzschild solved them exactly for the space and time outside a single, non-spinning spherical mass[1]. His solution has a special radius, $r_s = 2GM/c^2$. When mass is compressed inside its Schwarzschild radius, nothing that crosses that sphere, not even light, can get back out. In 1939 Oppenheimer and Snyder showed, for an idealised star with no pressure left to hold it up, that it collapses through its own Schwarzschild radius[2]. In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope published an image of the black hole at the centre of the galaxy M87[3]: a bright ring of light around a dark centre. That dark centre is the black hole's shadow, which is more than twice the size of the horizon.

How Light Moves Near a Black Hole

Far from any mass, light travels in straight lines. Near a mass, general relativity says it follows the straightest possible path through curved spacetime, a null geodesic. For Schwarzschild's spacetime the shape of that path can be written exactly. With $r$ the distance from the centre (Schwarzschild's radial coordinate), $\varphi$ the angle around the hole and $u = 1/r$, every light ray obeys[5]

$$\frac{d^2u}{d\varphi^2} = -u + \frac{3GM}{c^2}\,u^2$$

Without the last term this is the equation of a straight line. The last term is gravity, and it is not an approximation: the simulation integrates this equation step by step (fourth-order Runge–Kutta in $\varphi$), starting from a ray that comes in from very far away with impact parameter $b$. Nothing about the answer is put in by hand; capture, wrapping and bending all come out of the integration.

The Photon Sphere and the Capture Radius

Multiplying the equation by $du/d\varphi$ and integrating once gives

$$\left(\frac{du}{d\varphi}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{b^2} - u^2\left(1 - \frac{2GM}{c^2}u\right)$$

The ray turns around where the right-hand side reaches zero. The bracketed term $u^2(1 - 2GMu/c^2)$ has a maximum at $u = c^2/(3GM)$, that is at $r = 3GM/c^2 = 1.5\,r_s$. At that radius light can circle the hole: this is the photon sphere, the dashed gold circle. The orbit there is unstable: a ray with $b$ almost exactly at the critical value below circles the hole several times before it either leaves or falls in. The maximum value of the bracket is $(c^2/GM)^2/27$, so a ray turns around only if

$$b > b_c = 3\sqrt{3}\,\frac{GM}{c^2} = \sqrt{27}\,\frac{GM}{c^2} \approx 2.598\,r_s$$

Every ray with a smaller impact parameter passes the photon sphere and crosses the horizon. Press "Skim the photon sphere" to set $b$ one part in $10^5$ above $b_c$: the ray is bent by about 637°, wrapping around the hole almost twice before it leaves. Moving the slider back and forth across 2.60 and 2.59 shows the switch from escaping to captured. Finding that switch by bisection with the simulation's own integrator gives $b_c = 2.5980762\,r_s$, equal to $\sqrt{27}\,GM/c^2$ to about one part in 1013.

The Shadow Is Not the Horizon

Run the light backwards. A camera far away looks towards the hole; each direction it looks in corresponds to a ray with some impact parameter. Rays with $b < b_c$ end on the horizon, so nothing can arrive from those directions. The camera sees a dark disk of radius $b_c \approx 2.6\,r_s$, the shadow, set by the photon sphere and not by the horizon. The horizon, at $r_s$, is itself never seen. The beam view shows the same disk from the other side: every ray inside the red band is captured.

The Event Horizon Telescope observed the hot gas around two black holes at a wavelength of 1.3 mm. For M87* it found an emission ring 42 ± 3 µas (millionths of an arcsecond) across[3]; for Sgr A*, at the centre of the Milky Way, a ring 51.8 ± 2.3 µas across[4]. What these images show is light from gas, bent around the photon sphere, framing the shadow. The ring is not the horizon, and its exact size depends on where the gas glows, which the EHT calibrated with simulations[3].

The scale buttons turn the picture into real sizes. For Sgr A* the mass, about $4 \times 10^6$ solar masses, was known from the orbits of stars around it before the image[4]; with the distance of 8178 pc measured from one of those stars[6], a non-spinning black hole would cast a shadow about 50 µas across, close to the ring that was imaged. For M87* the EHT went the other way and inferred the mass, $(6.5 \pm 0.7) \times 10^9$ solar masses at 16.8 Mpc, from the ring itself[3], so the 39.7 µas the formula gives for that mass is a consistency check rather than a test.

Far from the Hole: Einstein's 4GM/(c²b)

When $b$ is much larger than $r_s$, the ray passes far outside the photon sphere and bends only slightly. To first order in $GM/(c^2 b)$ the deflection angle is

$$\delta \approx \frac{4GM}{c^2 b}$$

the value Einstein obtained in 1915[7]. For light grazing the Sun it is 1.75″, and the 1919 eclipse expeditions found deflections consistent with it[8]. Press "Far ray" to set $b = 50\,r_s$: the simulation's ray bends by 2.362°, while the formula gives 2.292°. The 3 % difference is real, not numerical error. The next term in the expansion, $\tfrac{15\pi}{4}\,(GM/c^2 b)^2$[9], accounts for it: at $b = 50\,r_s$ it adds 0.068° and brings the series within 0.003° of the integrated ray. The first-order formula is within 2 % of the exact bending only beyond about 75 $r_s$, and within 1 % beyond about 150 $r_s$. Near $b_c$ it fails completely: at $b = 3\,r_s$ the ray bends by 98.5°, against 38.2° from the formula.

When Black Holes Collide

In 1971 Stephen Hawking proved that in classical general relativity the total area of black-hole horizons can never decrease[10]. For a non-spinning hole the area is $A = 4\pi r_s^2 = 16\pi G^2M^2/c^4$; for a spinning one it also depends on the spin. When two black holes merge, the remnant must have more horizon area than the two had together, even though the merger radiates away mass-energy as gravitational waves. Isi and collaborators tested this with the public data of GW150914[11][12], finding that the area had grown with 97 % probability. With the much louder signal GW250114, the LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA collaborations confirmed that the remnant's area is larger than the sum of the initial areas, to high credibility[13].

What the Simulation Leaves Out

The black hole here does not spin. Real astrophysical black holes do, and a spinning (Kerr) black hole casts a shadow of slightly different size and, seen from most angles, slightly flattened shape. The picture is drawn in Schwarzschild's coordinates $r$ and $\varphi$ laid flat on the screen; that is a choice of map, not what an observer would photograph. The moving dot travels at a steady pace along the path so the shape is easy to follow; it does not show the timing of the light, which in these coordinates seems to slow down near the horizon. There is no glowing gas: the bright ring the EHT sees would come from light emitted around the hole and bent on its way to us, which needs a model of that gas.

Key Takeaway
  • When mass is compressed inside its Schwarzschild radius $r_s = 2GM/c^2$, not even light can escape: 2.95 km for the mass of the Sun, 8.9 mm for the Earth.
  • Light can circle the hole on the photon sphere at $1.5\,r_s$. Rays with impact parameter below $b_c = \sqrt{27}\,GM/c^2$, about $2.6\,r_s$, are captured.
  • We now have images of the shadow a black hole casts on the light around it. The shadow is set by $b_c$; the horizon itself is not seen.

$$b_c = 3\sqrt{3}\,\frac{GM}{c^2}$$

$$\delta \approx \frac{4GM}{c^2 b}\quad (b \gg r_s)$$
