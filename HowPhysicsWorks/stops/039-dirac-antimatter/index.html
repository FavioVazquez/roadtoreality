Dirac and Antimatter

For every particle, there is an antiparticle

1928 · Paul Dirac

In 1928 Paul Dirac wrote an equation for the electron that obeys special relativity[1]. It had solutions with negative energy that would not go away. In 1931 he proposed that they point to a new particle, an "anti-electron" with the electron's mass and the opposite charge[2][8]. In 1932 Carl Anderson reported such particles in cosmic rays[3].

A Relativistic Equation for the Electron

Schrödinger's equation treats time and space differently, so it cannot be right for fast particles. Dirac looked for an equation that is first order in both, and found one that works only if the wavefunction has four components[1]. The electron's spin, which Pauli had added by hand, appears on its own[7].

The price was a set of solutions with negative energy. An electron in such a state would have energy below $-m_ec^2$, and nothing in the equation stopped an ordinary electron from falling into one.

The Anti-electron

In 1930 Dirac tried to identify the empty negative-energy states, the "holes", with protons. Robert Oppenheimer showed that ordinary atoms would then annihilate almost at once, and Hermann Weyl that the holes must have the electron's own mass. In 1931 Dirac accepted this and predicted a particle not yet seen: an "anti-electron" with the same mass as the electron and the opposite charge, which would annihilate when it met an electron[2][8].

Energy Bookkeeping

When an electron and a positron at rest annihilate, their rest energy, $2m_ec^2$, becomes light. Total momentum is zero before, so it must be zero after: one photon cannot do that, but two photons going in opposite directions can, each carrying $m_ec^2 = 510.999$ keV[6][7].

The reverse also happens. A photon with at least $2m_ec^2 \approx 1.022$ MeV can turn into an electron and a positron, but only near another charge, in practice a nucleus. A photon of energy $E$ carries momentum $E/c$, while a pair with the same total energy can carry at most $\sqrt{E^2 - (2m_ec^2)^2}/c$. Something else has to take up the difference, and a nearby nucleus does, with almost no energy of its own. The Pair production view computes that missing momentum.

Reading the Track

Anderson was studying cosmic rays with a cloud chamber inside a magnet[5]. A charged particle leaves a trail of droplets, and the field bends the trail into a circle of radius

$$r = \frac{p}{|q|\,B}$$

A track bending one way could be a positive particle going up or a negative one coming down. To tell which, the chamber had a lead plate across the middle, 6 mm thick[5]. A particle loses momentum crossing the plate, so its track curls more tightly on the far side. That fixes the direction of travel, and then the sense of the bend fixes the sign of the charge. How tightly it curled, together with how densely it ionised the gas, showed the mass was far below a proton's. In his 1933 paper Anderson reported 15 tracks of positive particles among 1300 cosmic-ray photographs and concluded that their mass was less than twenty times the electron's[4].

What the Simulation Assumes

The photon and particle energies come from $E = mc^2$ and momentum conservation, with the CODATA electron mass. In the pair view the kinetic energy is drawn split equally between the two particles, which is a display choice; real pairs share it unequally. In the cloud chamber the field (1.5 T), the 30 cm chamber and the 40 % of the momentum kept after the plate are illustrative, not Anderson's values. The radii follow from $r = p/(|q|B)$.

Key Takeaway
  • Dirac's 1928 equation had negative-energy solutions; in 1931 he read them as an anti-electron of the same mass and opposite charge.
  • Annihilation at rest gives two back-to-back photons of 511 keV; a photon needs at least 1.022 MeV and a nearby nucleus to make a pair.
  • A curved track that loses momentum in a plate gives direction, charge and a mass limit: Anderson's evidence for the positron (1932–33).

$$e^- + e^+ \to \gamma + \gamma, \qquad E_\gamma \ge 2m_ec^2 = 1.022\ \text{MeV}$$
