The Standard Model

The periodic table of fundamental particles

1960s–1970s · Glashow, Weinberg & Salam

The question

Which forces does each of the seventeen particles feel, and how far apart are their masses?

The Standard Model is the theory of the particles that make up matter and of three of the four forces: the strong, the weak and the electromagnetic[6]. Its electroweak part was set out by Sheldon Glashow in 1961, Steven Weinberg in 1967 and Abdus Salam in 1968[1]–[3].

Seventeen Particles

Matter is made of fermions, which have spin 1/2. There are six quarks and six leptons, arranged in three generations. Each generation repeats the one before with the same charges and heavier masses: the muon is a heavier copy of the electron, the tau a heavier copy again[4],[10]. Up-type quarks carry charge +2/3, down-type quarks −1/3, the electron, muon and tau −1, and the neutrinos none.

The forces are carried by spin-1 bosons: the gluon for the strong force, the photon for electromagnetism, and the W and Z for the weak force[6]. The seventeenth particle is the Higgs boson, with spin 0[4]. The count of seventeen lists each kind once: it does not count antiparticles, the three colours of each quark or the eight gluons separately, and it counts W+ and W− as one entry.

Masses Over Eleven Orders of Magnitude

The heaviest particle, the top quark at 172.57 GeV, is about 340,000 times heavier than the electron at 0.511 MeV[4]. The neutrinos are lighter still: tritium decay limits the neutrino mass to below 0.8 eV[4]. From that bound to the top quark is a factor of more than 2 × 1011, so the axis in the simulation is logarithmic. The photon and gluon are massless.

Quarks are never seen alone, so their masses are not weighed directly. The light-quark masses are values of a parameter in the theory at a stated energy scale (2 GeV for up, down and strange); the top-quark mass is from direct measurements of its decays[4].

Who Feels What

Quarks feel all three forces. The charged leptons feel electromagnetism and the weak force but not the strong force. Neutrinos have no charge and feel only the weak force. The photon couples to electric charge, the gluon to the strong charge called colour, and the W, being charged, feels electromagnetism as well as the weak force[10].

In the electroweak theory the Higgs field has a value of $v = 246.22$ GeV throughout empty space, and each fermion's coupling to the Higgs boson is proportional to its mass, $g\,m_f/2M_W$[5]. That is what the "Higgs" chip in the table means: the particle gets its mass from this coupling.

Switch Off a Force

The force switches show which interactions a particle would lose. Switch off the strong force and quarks no longer bind into protons and neutrons, so there are no nuclei. Switch off electromagnetism and there is no light, and nothing holds electrons to nuclei, so there are no atoms. Switch off the weak force and there is no beta decay, and the first step of hydrogen burning in the Sun, two protons fusing to deuterium, cannot happen because it turns a proton into a neutron[10].

The Higgs Does Not Make Most of Your Mass

The Higgs field gives mass to the elementary particles, but not to most of the matter around you. A proton is two up quarks and a down quark, whose masses add up to about 9 MeV[4]. The proton's mass-energy is 938.272 MeV[9]. A lattice calculation of the strong interaction finds the proton's mass split into the energy of the moving quarks (33 %), the energy of the gluon field (37 %), a quantum effect called the trace anomaly (23 %), and only about 9 % from the quark masses themselves[8]. So most of the mass of protons and neutrons, and of you, is strong-interaction energy. The Higgs switch in the simulation says so.

What It Leaves Out

The Standard Model does not include gravity: its forces are the strong, weak and electromagnetic ones[6]. It has no particle for the dark matter that cosmology needs: the Standard Model's neutrinos account for between 0.5 % and 1.6 % of the non-baryonic matter in the universe, and the rest is unknown[7]. And within the Standard Model neutrinos are exactly massless, because it has no right-handed neutrinos to build a mass term from. Neutrino oscillations show that they do have mass, so the model has to be extended[6].

What the Simulation Assumes

Each kind of particle is shown once. The neutrinos are drawn at the 0.8 eV bound, which is an upper limit, not a measured mass. The force switches are thought experiments: they list what would be lost, not a calculation of a universe without that force.

Key Takeaway
  • Seventeen kinds of particle: six quarks and six leptons in three generations, four kinds of force carrier and the Higgs boson.
  • Their masses span more than eleven orders of magnitude; each fermion's coupling to the Higgs is proportional to its mass.
  • The Higgs gives the elementary particles their masses, but most of a proton's mass is strong-interaction energy.
  • Gravity, dark matter and neutrino masses lie outside the model.
