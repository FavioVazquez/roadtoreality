Schrödinger's Wave Equation

The probability cloud that replaced the orbit

1926 · Erwin Schrödinger

In 1926 Erwin Schrödinger published a wave equation for matter[1]. It does not give a particle's path. It gives a wavefunction $\psi$, and later that year Max Born proposed that $|\psi|^2$ is the probability of finding the particle at each place[3].

A Wave Equation for Matter

De Broglie had given electrons a wavelength. Schrödinger asked what equation such a wave obeys. For one particle of mass $m$ moving along a line in a potential $V(x)$ his time-dependent equation reads[2][6]

$$i\hbar\,\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2} + V(x)\,\psi$$

In the first of his 1926 papers he solved the time-independent form for the hydrogen atom and recovered its energy levels[1]. Heisenberg, Born and Jordan had reached the same levels a few months earlier with matrix mechanics; Schrödinger showed that the two theories give the same predictions[4].

What $\psi$ Means

The wavefunction is a complex number at each point, with a size and a phase. Schrödinger at first hoped it described a spread-out electron. Born's reading, which became the standard one, is that $|\psi(x)|^2$ is a probability density: a position measurement finds the electron at one place, and over many repeats the results are distributed as $|\psi|^2$[3]. In the simulation the height of the coloured area is $|\psi|^2$ and its colour is the phase.

An Electron in a Box

The simplest case is a particle between two walls it cannot pass, with $V = 0$ inside. The states of fixed energy are standing waves that fit the box[6]:

$$\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{L}}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \qquad E_n = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8 m L^2}$$

For an electron in a box $L = 1$ nm wide, the CODATA constants[7] give $E_1 \approx 0.376$ eV. Each state's wavefunction only turns its phase in time, at a rate $E_n/\hbar$, so $|\psi_n|^2$ never changes. That is why these are called stationary states. State $n$ has $n - 1$ nodes, points inside the box where $\psi$ is always zero. Choose One state, move the $n$ slider and count them; the colour cycles while the shape stays still.

Superpositions Move

Any other state is a sum of stationary states, $\psi(x,t) = \sum_n c_n\,\psi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}$. Because the phases turn at different rates, the parts drift in and out of step and $|\psi|^2$ moves. An equal mix of $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ sloshes from side to side with period $h/(E_2 - E_1) \approx 3.7$ fs.

A localised wave packet needs many terms. It travels, reflects off the walls and spreads into a complicated interference pattern. In this box every energy is a whole-number multiple of $E_1$, so after $t = h/E_1 \approx 11$ fs every phase is back where it started and the packet returns exactly to its original shape, a quantum revival[5]. Halfway there, at about 5.5 fs, it reappears mirrored. Revivals of this kind have been observed in electron wave packets in Rydberg atoms[8].

The Cloud That Replaced the Orbit

For the hydrogen atom the same equation, with the electron held by the proton's Coulomb pull, gives the energies $E_n = -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$, the levels of Bohr's model[1][7]. But the states are not orbits. They are three-dimensional clouds of probability[6]. The ground state, 1s, is a ball with no edge; the chance of finding the electron at distance $r$ peaks at the Bohr radius $a_0 \approx 0.0529$ nm[7], exactly where Bohr put his orbit. The 2s state has a spherical node, and 2p (here the one with $m = 0$) has a nodal plane through the nucleus. In this model 2s and 2p have the same energy. The dashed circle and line mark Bohr's orbit radius $n^2 a_0$ for comparison.

How the Simulation Works

The sim adds up the stationary states with their exact phase factors, so there is no time-stepping error. The packet is a Gaussian (width 0.07 nm, starting at 0.3 nm, moving right) expanded on the lowest 80 states; the readout shows how much of the state those 80 terms capture. The model is one-dimensional and non-relativistic, with perfectly hard walls. Time runs about $10^{15}$ times slower than in the model, at the playback speed shown. The hydrogen view uses the exact non-relativistic 1s, 2s and 2p wavefunctions in a slice through the nucleus; for visibility the brightness is the square root of the density, and the colour shows the phase, which for a stationary state only turns. $\langle x \rangle$ and $\langle E \rangle$ are averages calculated from $\psi$, not measurements.

Key Takeaway
  • The Schrödinger equation (1926) fixes how the wavefunction $\psi$ changes in time; $|\psi|^2$ gives the probabilities of where the particle is found (Born, 1926).
  • Stationary states have fixed $|\psi|^2$; for an electron in a 1 nm box the lowest energy is about 0.376 eV.
  • Superpositions move because their parts turn phase at different rates; in a box a packet revives exactly after $h/E_1$.
  • In hydrogen the orbit becomes a cloud: the 1s probability peaks at the Bohr radius, but the electron has no path.

$$i\hbar\,\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = \hat H\,\psi$$
