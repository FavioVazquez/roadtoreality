Bohr's Model of the Atom

Electrons jump between fixed energy levels

1913 · Niels Bohr

The question

Why should a classical atom collapse, and which electron jumps in Bohr's hydrogen atom give its visible spectral lines?

Bohr proposed that electrons can only occupy specific energy levels — and emit light when they jump between them. Each element has a unique spectral fingerprint.

The Classical Prediction: Catastrophe

An electron orbiting a nucleus is an accelerating charge. Accelerating charges radiate energy (Stop 025 — Hertz). As the electron radiates, it loses energy and spirals inward, eventually crashing into the nucleus in about ten picoseconds. Classical physics predicts every atom should instantly collapse. Watch the simulation — it does.

Bohr's Radical Proposal

In 1913, Niels Bohr simply declared that electrons in atoms do not obey classical radiation laws. They can only occupy specific quantized orbits, each corresponding to a discrete energy level. An electron in a Bohr orbit does not radiate. The atom is stable by decree.

What made physicists take the decree seriously was arithmetic: from one new rule, that the electron's angular momentum comes in multiples of h/2π, Bohr derived the energy levels below and with them Rydberg's constant, which until then was a number fitted to the spectrum, from e, the electron's mass and h alone. With the constants as known in 1913 it came out about 6 % low, within their uncertainty (Bohr quoted 3.1 × 10¹⁵ against the observed 3.29 × 10¹⁵ s⁻¹), and within 1 % as the constants improved; the model then predicted lines not yet seen in hydrogen and in ionised helium.

The Emission Spectrum as Proof

When an electron jumps from a higher orbit to a lower one, it emits a photon with exactly the energy difference. The photon's frequency — and therefore its color — is determined by which two levels are involved. Every element has its own set of levels and so its own spectral fingerprint; Bohr's formula gives them exactly only for hydrogen and one-electron ions, and the rest had to wait for the full quantum theory three stops on. Hydrogen's Balmer series (jumps ending on n = 2) falls in the visible range.

What the Simulation Leaves Out

The classical collapse is slowed from about ten picoseconds, as the text gives, to 3.5 seconds, and its radius shrinks along a simple exponential spiral chosen for display, not the exact classical decay. Only levels n = 1 to 6 are drawn, with radii and orbital speeds compressed for clicking; the hint under the button gives the real n² and 1/n³ scalings. Photon wavelengths come from λ = 1240 nm·eV / ΔE with Eₙ = −13.6 eV/n², and the spectrum bar covers only 380 to 700 nm, so ultraviolet and infrared photons get a tag instead of a line.

Key Equations

$$E_n = -\frac{13.6 \text{ eV}}{n^2}$$

$$\Delta E = h\nu = 13.6\left(\frac{1}{n_{\text{low}}^2} - \frac{1}{n_{\text{high}}^2}\right) \text{ eV}$$

The Balmer series ($n \to 2$): H$\alpha$ = 656 nm (red), H$\beta$ = 486 nm (cyan), H$\gamma$ = 434 nm (violet), H$\delta$ = 410 nm (deep violet).
