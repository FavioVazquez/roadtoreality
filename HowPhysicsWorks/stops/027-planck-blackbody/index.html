Energy comes in chunks — the birth of quantum theory

The question

Where Wien's Law Broke

By 1900 Wilhelm Wien's formula fitted the glow of hot bodies well at short wavelengths, but new far-infrared measurements by Heinrich Rubens and Ferdinand Kurlbaum in Berlin showed it failing at long wavelengths, where the measured radiance kept growing with temperature instead of saturating.[1][2] The classical formula that would later be called the Rayleigh–Jeans law gets that long-wavelength end right and then grows without limit at high frequencies, a failure Paul Ehrenfest named the ultraviolet catastrophe in 1911.[3][4] Planck's formula, below, fits both ends; the dashed classical curve in the simulation shows where it departs.

Planck's Desperate Act

In 1900, Max Planck found the correct formula by assuming that the energy of the tiny oscillators in the cavity walls could be counted in equal elements ε = hf, where h is now called Planck's constant. He did not yet say that light itself comes in packets. Planck later described the step as "an act of desperation" (letter to R. W. Wood, 1931): he wanted a formula that fitted, and quantized energy was the only way he could get one. He spent years trying to reconcile it with classical physics. It took Einstein, in 1905, to take the quantum literally.

Wien's Displacement Law

The peak of the blackbody spectrum shifts to shorter wavelengths as temperature increases: λmax = 2,898 µm·K / T. Hot stars are blue-white; cooler stars are red. Our Sun, at 5,772 K, peaks near 500 nm (green) when the spectrum is plotted per unit wavelength, which is how the simulation shows it; plotted per unit frequency the same curve peaks in the near infrared, a well-known subtlety. In the simulation the axes are fixed, so you can drag the temperature slider and watch the peak move from 2.9 µm at 1,000 K, through 724 nm at 4,000 K, to 290 nm at 10,000 K, while the area under the curve rises as T4 and the peak height as T5.

What the Simulation Leaves Out

The vertical axis is radiance per unit wavelength on a fixed log scale, relative to the peak at 10,000 K, with the common factor 2hc² dropped, so the values are ratios, not radiances in physical units. The wavelength window runs from 40 nm to 3 µm, and the curve colour switches between three fixed tints (below 3,000 K, 3,000 to 6,000 K, above 6,000 K) rather than showing the true colour of the glow. While playing, the temperature swings up to 1,500 K either side of the slider setting, within 1,000 to 10,000 K, except for about three seconds after you move the slider or press the Sun button, when it holds the setting so you can read the peak; the Sun button sets 5,772 K and the slider follows it exactly. Shrinking h to a hundredth of its value is a thought experiment: h is a fixed constant of nature.

Key Equations

$$E = hf$$

$$B(\nu, T) = \frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2} \cdot \frac{1}{e^{h\nu/k_BT} - 1}$$

When \(h \to 0\), Planck's formula reduces to the classical Rayleigh-Jeans law \(B \propto \nu^2 T\). Drag the h slider to the left to see both curves converge.

(\(k_B\) is Boltzmann's constant, \(c\) the speed of light.)
