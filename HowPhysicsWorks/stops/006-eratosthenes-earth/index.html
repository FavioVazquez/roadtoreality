Eratosthenes Measures the Earth

A stick, a shadow, and the size of a world

~240 BCE · Eratosthenes of Cyrene

Eratosthenes was the chief librarian of Alexandria. He knew that at Syene (modern Aswan), at noon on the summer solstice, a vertical gnomon cast no shadow (the famous well comes from later retellings). In Alexandria, 5,000 stadia to the north by the surveyors' reckoning, roughly 800 km, a vertical stick did cast a shadow that day. The only detailed ancient account is Cleomedes' De motu circulari I.7; Strabo and Pliny mention the result.

He realized this could only happen on a curved surface. By measuring the angle of Alexandria's shadow and knowing the distance between the cities, he calculated Earth's circumference. His answer: 250,000 stadia (252,000 in some sources). How long his stadion was is not known; on the most likely value (about 157.5 m) that is 39,400–39,700 km, within 2% of today's 40,008 km around the poles; on the longest ancient stadion it would be 16% too large. Either way: the right size of a planet, from a shadow.

The Geometry

The key insight: if the Earth is spherical, parallel rays of sunlight hit different latitudes at different angles. In Syene (then on the Tropic of Cancer at the solstice), the sun is directly overhead — angle 0°. In Alexandria, the same sunlight hits at an angle of about 7.2°.

That 7.2° is 1/50th of a full 360° circle. So if the distance between the cities (in round numbers, 800 km) equals 1/50th of the circumference, the full circumference must be 50 × 800 = 40,000 km. That's all the mathematics required.

Eratosthenes lived before calculators, telescopes, or any precision instruments beyond a stick and a measuring cord. Later Greek estimates were smaller (Posidonius' 180,000 stadia, adopted by Ptolemy), and that smaller Earth was the one Columbus believed in. Eratosthenes' figure was the better one, but not the one that was taught.

Key Takeaway
  • Eratosthenes used the shadow angle difference between two cities to measure Earth's circumference — and got the Earth's size right to within a few per cent on the most favourable reading of his units, and to within 16% on the least favourable.
  • The only inputs he needed: the shadow angle at one city, and the distance between the two cities.
  • This is one of the most elegant experiments in history: profound result from the simplest possible observation.

Sources

  1. Cleomedes, De motu circulari corporum caelestium I.7 (the only detailed ancient account of the measurement: 250,000 stadia).
  2. Strabo, Geography II.5.7, and Pliny the Elder, Natural History II.247 (252,000 stadia).
  3. The length of the stadion is not known; values from about 157.5 m (Egyptian) to 185 m (Attic) are discussed in the literature, giving roughly 39,400–39,700 km and 46,250–46,620 km. Today's meridional circumference, 40,008 km, is the figure used for comparison.
