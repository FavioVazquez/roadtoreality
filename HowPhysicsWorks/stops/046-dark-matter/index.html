Dark Matter

Most of the matter in the universe is not made of atoms

1970s–present · Vera Rubin

The question

Can the stars and gas of NGC 3198 explain its flat rotation curve, and what does a dark halo fit actually pin down?

In 1933 Fritz Zwicky measured how fast the galaxies of the Coma cluster move and found them far too fast to be held together by the mass their light suggested[1]. Nearly forty years later the same problem turned up inside single galaxies. Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured the rotation of the Andromeda galaxy from its glowing gas clouds[2]; Albert Bosma followed the neutral hydrogen of spiral galaxies with radio telescopes, far beyond the edge of their starlight[3]; and Rubin, Ford and Norbert Thonnard measured 21 spirals[4]. In galaxy after galaxy the rotation speed stays high, the curve flat, far out where the stars and gas thin out. If gravity is Newton's, a flat curve needs mass that we do not see.

The simulation builds the rotation curve of the spiral galaxy NGC 3198 from its parts, and lays it over the curve astronomers observed. It starts with the stars and gas alone. Add a dark halo, adjust it until the curve fits, and watch what the fit can and cannot tell you.

Dark halo

What a Rotation Curve Tells You

A star or gas cloud on a circular orbit at radius $r$ moves at the speed $v$ for which gravity supplies exactly the needed inward pull. For a spherical distribution of mass this gives

$$v(r)^2 = \frac{G\,M(<r)}{r}$$

where $M(<r)$ is the mass inside the orbit. Outside most of the mass, $M$ stops growing and $v$ falls as $1/\sqrt{r}$, as the planets do around the Sun. A flat curve, $v$ constant, needs $M(<r)$ to keep growing in proportion to $r$. The galaxy view on the left shows what that means in motion: the hollow circles go round at the observed speed, the filled dots at the model's. When the model is short of mass, the outer dots fall behind.

The Galaxy and Its Observed Curve

NGC 3198 is a spiral galaxy whose rotation curve is unusually well measured and extends far beyond its stars, which made it a classic test case in the 1980s[5][6]. The 43 points in the plot are the observed curve as tabulated in the SPARC database of Lelli, McGaugh and Schombert[7], from hydrogen-line and Hα velocities (they cite Begeman 1987 and 1991 and Daigle et al. 2006), with every radius placed at SPARC's distance of 13.8 Mpc from Cepheid stars. Older papers used a smaller distance, so their radii are smaller. The error bars are SPARC's: the fit error combined with the difference between the two sides of the disk; they do not include the uncertainty in the galaxy's tilt. The table is copied into the page unchanged.

Building the Visible Galaxy

The model adds the squares of the speeds that each component would produce on its own:

$$v^2 = v_{\text{bulge}}^2 + v_{\text{disk}}^2 + v_{\text{gas}}\lvert v_{\text{gas}}\rvert + v_{\text{halo}}^2$$

Stars. The stellar disk is a thin exponential disk with surface density $\Sigma(R) = \Sigma_0 e^{-R/R_d}$ and SPARC's scale length $R_d = 3.14$ kpc[7]. A flat disk does not pull like a point mass, and its exact rotation speed was worked out by Freeman[8]:

$$v_{\text{disk}}^2 = 4\pi G\Sigma_0 R_d\, y^2\left[I_0(y)K_0(y) - I_1(y)K_1(y)\right]$$

with $y = R/(2R_d)$ and $I_n$, $K_n$ modified Bessel functions. It peaks at $2.2\,R_d$ about 8 % below the speed of a point with the same mass. The disk's mass is its 3.6 µm luminosity, $3.83 \times 10^{10}$ solar luminosities[7], times the mass-to-light ratio $\Upsilon_\star$. Starlight does not tell us $\Upsilon_\star$ precisely; the default 0.5 is the value SPARC adopts at this wavelength[7].

Gas. The gas contribution is SPARC's, computed from the observed map of neutral hydrogen and multiplied by 1.33 for helium[7]. It is not adjustable here. Near the centre it is slightly negative: a ring of gas outside an orbit pulls outward.

Bulge. SPARC finds no bulge in NGC 3198, so the default is zero. The slider adds a Hernquist bulge[9], $v^2 = GM_b r/(r+a)^2$ with $a = 0.5$ kpc, for experiments.

With stars and gas alone the model curve rises to about 100 km/s near 7 kpc and then falls; at 44 kpc it is 65 km/s against an observed 149 km/s. At $\Upsilon_\star = 0.5$ it lies below every observed point beyond 2.2 kpc. A heavier disk helps only near the centre: even at the slider's largest value, $\Upsilon_\star = 1.2$, the model is below every point beyond 10 kpc.

Adding a Dark Halo

Two halo shapes are offered. The pseudo-isothermal halo has a constant-density core, $\rho = \rho_0/[1 + (r/r_c)^2]$, used for NGC 3198 by van Albada and collaborators[5]:

$$v_{\text{halo}}^2 = v_\infty^2\left[1 - \frac{r_c}{r}\arctan\frac{r}{r_c}\right]$$

where $v_\infty^2 = 4\pi G\rho_0 r_c^2$. The NFW halo is the shape that dark-matter halos take in cold-dark-matter simulations of structure formation[10], $\rho \propto 1/[(r/r_s)(1 + r/r_s)^2]$ with $r_s = r_{200}/c$:

$$v_{\text{halo}}^2 = V_{200}^2\,\frac{\ln(1+cx) - cx/(1+cx)}{x\left[\ln(1+c) - c/(1+c)\right]}$$

with $x = r/r_{200}$, $r_{200} = V_{200}/(10\,H_0)$ and $H_0 = 73$ km/s/Mpc, the value SPARC uses[7]. The fit quality is

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{43}\left(\frac{v_{\text{model}}(r_i) - v_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^2$$

with $\nu = N - k$ and $k$ the number of halo parameters adjusted (2, or 0 with no halo). Press the fit button: it finds the two halo parameters that minimise $\chi^2$ for the stars and bulge you have set. At the default $\Upsilon_\star = 0.5$ the simulation's best pseudo-isothermal halo has $v_\infty = 143.8$ km/s and $r_c = 2.98$ kpc, with $\chi^2/\nu = 1.67$ and an rms difference of 4.9 km/s; its best NFW halo has $V_{200} = 114$ km/s and $c = 8.7$, with $\chi^2/\nu = 2.57$ and 8.1 km/s rms. Inside the last point at 44 kpc the fitted halo holds about $1.9 \times 10^{11}$ solar masses, roughly six times the stars and gas together. These are fits of this page's model, not values from the literature.

What the Curve Cannot Tell You

The rotation curve demands extra mass, at the latest from 10 kpc outward. It does not say how that mass is split between the disk and the halo, or what shape the halo has in the middle. Change $\Upsilon_\star$ and press "Fit the halo" again: with the pseudo-isothermal halo, $\Upsilon_\star = 0.35$, 0.5 and 0.65 give $\chi^2/\nu$ of 2.6, 1.7 and 1.7, each with a different core radius (2.0, 3.0 and 4.5 kpc). A heavier disk is traded for a halo with a larger core. Van Albada and collaborators pointed out exactly this for NGC 3198 in 1985[5]. Whether real halos have cores, as the pseudo-isothermal shape assumes, or the steep central cusps of the NFW shape is still argued over, galaxy by galaxy[11][12].

The model also has limits of its own. The exponential disk is a smooth stand-in for the measured light. "Compare SPARC's disk from the light" draws SPARC's own disk curve, computed from the observed light profile[7]: inside about 5 kpc the two differ by up to about 25 km/s, because the galaxy is not a perfect exponential, and that is where the fits here are worst. Rotation curves like this one can also be fitted with no dark matter at all, by changing Newton's law at very small accelerations (MOND)[13]; Begeman, Broeils and Sanders did this for NGC 3198 among other galaxies[16]. That is why the other evidence matters.

The Other Evidence

Two independent lines of evidence point the same way. Gravitational lensing of the colliding galaxy cluster 1E 0657−558, the Bullet Cluster, shows the mass concentrated where the galaxies are, offset at 8σ from the hot gas that holds most of the ordinary matter[14]. The pattern of peaks in the cosmic microwave background measures the density of ordinary matter and of matter that does not interact with light separately: Planck finds $\Omega_c h^2 = 0.120 \pm 0.001$ for dark matter and $\Omega_b h^2 = 0.0224 \pm 0.0001$ for ordinary (baryonic) matter[15]. From these two cited numbers, about 84 % of the matter in the universe is not made of atoms. What that matter is remains unknown.

Key Takeaway
  • Galaxy rotation curves stay flat far beyond the starlight. With Newton's gravity, the stars and gas of NGC 3198 fall short of the observed speeds beyond 10 kpc for every stellar mass-to-light ratio the slider allows (up to 1.2).
  • A dark halo fits the observed curve, but the curve alone does not fix the halo's shape or how much of the mass is in the stars.
  • Lensing and the cosmic microwave background give independent evidence: about 84 % of the matter in the universe is not made of atoms (computed from Planck's cited densities).
