Nuclear Fission

Splitting the atom releases enormous energy

1938 · Hahn, Strassmann & Meitner

In December 1938 Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann bombarded uranium with slow neutrons and found barium among the products, an element about half as heavy[1][5]. Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch explained it: the uranium nucleus had split in two. Meitner estimated that each split releases about 200 MeV, and Frisch named the process fission[2][5].

Barium from Uranium

Through the 1930s several laboratories fired neutrons at uranium, expecting to make slightly heavier elements. Hahn and Strassmann, chemists at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Chemistry in Berlin, showed that one product behaved chemically as barium. They submitted the result to Die Naturwissenschaften on 22 December 1938[1][5]. Meitner and Frisch worked out that a uranium nucleus, pictured as a charged liquid drop, could stretch and split into two smaller nuclei, and published their account in Nature[2][5]. Later in 1939 Niels Bohr and John Wheeler gave the full liquid-drop theory of fission[3].

Where the Energy Comes From

The mass of a nucleus is less than the masses of its protons and neutrons; the difference, times $c^2$, is its binding energy. Divided by the number of nucleons it rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks in the iron–nickel region and falls slowly for heavy ones. Nickel-62 has the highest binding energy per nucleon, about 8.79 MeV; iron-56 has the lowest mass per nucleon[4][7]. Uranium-235 sits near 7.59 MeV per nucleon, its fragments near 8.3–8.5. Splitting it moves both halves up the curve, and the mass that disappears is released as energy. Light nuclei release energy the other way, by fusing.

The Where the energy comes from view computes this from the 2020 atomic mass evaluation[4]. For one common split,

$$^{235}\mathrm{U} + n \to {}^{141}\mathrm{Ba} + {}^{92}\mathrm{Kr} + 3n$$

the lost mass corresponds to 173.3 MeV, most of it the kinetic energy of the two fragments. The fragments are radioactive; once they have decayed to stable praseodymium-141 and zirconium-92 the total is 199.2 MeV, some of which escapes with neutrinos. This is the "about 200 MeV" Meitner estimated. One kilogram of uranium-235 split completely at 200 MeV per fission releases $8.2 \times 10^{13}$ J, about 20 kilotons of TNT, using the convention 1 ton of TNT = 4.184 GJ[8]. For comparison, the D–T fusion reaction releases 17.6 MeV from just five nucleons.

A Chain Reaction

A thermal-neutron fission of uranium-235 releases on average about 2.43 to 2.44 neutrons, depending on the evaluation[6]. If each of those has a chance $p$ of causing another fission, each generation is $k = \bar\nu\,p$ times the last. With $k = 1$ the chain holds steady; above 1 it grows, below 1 it dies out. In a reactor, $p$ is lowered by neutrons that are captured without fission, that leak out, or that are absorbed by control rods. In the simulation one slider stands for all three, and the bars show neutron counts drawn at random generation by generation.

Why a Reactor Can Be Controlled

Most neutrons appear within a tiny fraction of a second, and a neutron generation in a thermal reactor lasts of order $10^{-4}$ s[6]. If they were all there was, a reactor 0.1 % above critical would multiply its power by $e$ about every tenth of a second. But a fraction $\beta = 0.0065$ of the neutrons from uranium-235 are emitted from a fraction of a second to about a minute later, by decaying fission fragments[6]. As long as the excess reactivity $\rho = (k-1)/k$ stays below $\beta$, the power follows the slow delayed neutrons:

$$\frac{P(t)}{P_0} \approx \frac{\beta}{\beta-\rho}\,\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda\rho\,t}{\beta-\rho}\right)$$

with an effective one-group decay constant $\lambda \approx 0.077$ per second, the value that follows from the published delayed-neutron group data[6]. At $\rho = 0.1$ % that gives a doubling every 50 seconds or so instead of every 0.07 s, slow enough for control systems to follow. If $\rho$ reaches $\beta$, the reactor is "prompt critical" and the delayed neutrons no longer help.

What the Simulation Assumes

The chain view counts neutrons generation by generation, drawing 2 or 3 neutrons per fission with the right average; it ignores neutron energies, geometry and the fuel's changing state. The power curve is the one-group prompt-jump formula above, with no temperature feedback; it is a formula, not reactor data. The binding-energy curve plots, at each mass number, the most tightly bound nuclide in the mass table, and the Q values are calculated from tabulated atomic masses.

Key Takeaway
  • Uranium-235 split by a neutron releases about 200 MeV, from the mass lost as both fragments move up the binding-energy curve (calculated here from AME2020 masses).
  • Each fission frees about 2.4 neutrons, so the chain grows if more than about 41 % of them cause another fission.
  • The 0.65 % of neutrons that come late are what make a reactor controllable, as long as the reactivity stays below that fraction.

$$Q = \left[m(^{235}\mathrm{U}) + m_n - \textstyle\sum m_{\text{products}}\right]c^2 \approx 200\ \text{MeV}$$
