Archimedes and Buoyancy

Eureka! Why things float

~250 BCE · Archimedes of Syracuse

The story everyone knows: King Hiero II of Syracuse suspected his goldsmith of mixing silver into a gold wreath, and Archimedes, stepping into a bath, saw how to check it without melting it. The story is told by Vitruvius two centuries later (De architectura IX, preface); Archimedes himself never mentions it, and Galileo (La Bilancetta, 1586) pointed out that the water-overflow method as told is too crude to catch the fraud; a hydrostatic balance would do it.

Two separate facts are tangled in the legend. The bath shows the first: a submerged body displaces its own volume of water. Archimedes' principle is the second, and it is about weight: the water pushes up on the body with a force equal to the weight of the water displaced. This is the buoyant force.

The Principle

Archimedes' principle states: an object submerged in fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces. If that force is greater than the object's weight, the object floats. If less, it sinks.

This is why a massive steel ship floats — its hollow interior displaces far more water than its hull weighs. And why a solid steel ball sinks — it displaces less water than its weight requires.

In Vitruvius' telling he ran home naked shouting "Eureka!" (I have found it!), then showed that the wreath displaced more water than an equal weight of gold, so it was less dense. Whatever happened in the bath, the physics is in his own book: On Floating Bodies proves the buoyancy law as a theorem.

Key Takeaway
  • Buoyant force = weight of displaced fluid. If buoyancy exceeds gravity, the object floats.
  • What matters is density (mass per volume), not mass alone. A hollow steel ship floats; a solid steel ball sinks.
  • Archimedes proved it as a theorem (On Floating Bodies, Book I, Propositions 5–7, c. 250 BCE) from one postulate about how fluid under more pressure pushes fluid under less.

$$F_b = \rho_{\text{fluid}} \cdot V_{\text{displaced}} \cdot g$$
