Mass and energy are the same thing

1905 · Albert Einstein

The question

How much energy is a gram of mass equivalent to, and how does the total energy grow as that mass approaches light speed?

Einstein's most famous equation says that mass is concentrated energy. A paperclip's gram of matter holds about 9 × 10¹³ joules, enough to run a one-gigawatt city for a day, if it could all be released.

Mass Is Frozen Energy

A single gram of matter — a paperclip — holds the explosive energy of about 21 kilotons of TNT. The conversion factor is c², and c² is enormous: about 9 × 10¹⁶ joules per kilogram. This is why nuclear reactions release so much energy: even tiny amounts of mass convert to colossal amounts of energy. The equivalence is not a slogan: in 2005 the energy released when a nucleus captures a neutron was measured two ways, as the wavelength of the emitted gamma rays and as the mass difference of the nuclei in a trap, and the two agreed to four parts in ten million.

The Complete Equation

E = mc² is famous, but it is actually a special case. The full equation of Special Relativity is E² = (pc)² + (mc²)², where p is relativistic momentum p = γmv. When an object is at rest, p = 0, and the equation reduces to E = mc². When moving, the total energy is larger — the extra part is kinetic energy. Here m is the rest mass, the same in every frame; the old habit of a speed-dependent "relativistic mass" is not used on this page.

What the Velocity Slider Shows

The lower slider lets you give your mass a velocity. Watch how the total energy grows: the rest energy (mc²) stays fixed, while relativistic kinetic energy (γ−1)mc² adds on top. As v→c, kinetic energy grows without bound — which is why you can never accelerate a massive object to the speed of light.

What the Simulation Leaves Out

The sphere's radius follows a log scale from 1 kJ to about 9 × 10¹⁶ J, so a thousandfold rise in energy looks like modest growth, and the expanding rings are decoration. The energy bars are scaled to the total energy at 0.99c for whatever mass is set, so their proportions depend only on v. The comparisons use 1 t TNT = 4.184 × 10⁹ J and a city using 3.15 × 10¹⁶ J a year, about 1 GW. The readouts give the full rest energy, as if all of it could be released; the sim models no actual reaction.

Key Equations

$$E = mc^2$$

But the complete relativistic energy equation is:

$$E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$$

where $p = \gamma m v$ is relativistic momentum. $E = mc^2$ is the special case $p = 0$ — an object at rest.
