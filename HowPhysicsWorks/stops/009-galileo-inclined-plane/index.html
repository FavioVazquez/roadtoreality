Galileo's Inclined Plane

How to measure gravity without a stopwatch

1604 · Galileo Galilei

The problem with studying free fall is that objects fall too fast to measure precisely with Renaissance instruments. Galileo's solution was elegant: tilt the plane. By rolling balls down an inclined surface instead of dropping them, he slowed gravity enough to time carefully.

He found that distance traveled grows as the square of elapsed time — always, regardless of the ball's mass. This is uniformly accelerated motion, and it contradicted Aristotle completely.

The Discovery

Galileo's inclined plane experiments led him to the law of uniform acceleration: distance = ½at². In the Discorsi (1638) he describes timing the ball with a water clock: weigh the water that ran out during the roll. His working notes (Galileo MS 72, folio 107v) show distances at equal beats; Stillman Drake reconstructed the method as a plank with movable gut frets the ball clicked over, adjusted until the clicks fell on a steady rhythm. The notes give 33, 130, 298, 526, 824, 1192, 1600, 2104 units for beats 1 to 8: divide each by the square of the beat and you get 33 every time.

For a block sliding without friction the acceleration along the incline is g·sin θ: 4.9 m/s² at 30°, the full 9.8 m/s² at 90°. Galileo's balls rolled, and a rolling ball must also spin up, so it accelerates at only 5/7 of that, 3.5 m/s² at 30°. He could not have known: the distance still grows as t², and the ratio between two angles is unchanged. At every angle the result is the same for all masses; the 1 kg ball and the 10 kg ball reach the bottom together.

Medieval scholars had the mathematics of uniform acceleration (the Oxford Calculators' mean-speed theorem, Oresme's proof of it in the 1350s; Domingo de Soto said in 1551 that falling bodies accelerate uniformly). What they lacked was the measurement. Galileo supplied it, and after him physics argued with numbers.

Key Takeaway
  • On an inclined plane the acceleration is g·sin θ for a sliding block and (5/7) g·sin θ for a rolling ball; neither depends on the mass.
  • Distance traveled grows as the square of elapsed time: d = ½at². This is Galileo's law of uniform acceleration.
  • Galileo made the controlled experiment the arbiter: slow the motion down, vary one thing, measure, repeat.

$$d = \frac{1}{2} a t^2$$
