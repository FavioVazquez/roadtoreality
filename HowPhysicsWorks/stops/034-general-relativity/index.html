General Relativity: Curved Spacetime

Gravity is the curvature of space and time

1915 · Albert Einstein

Mercury's orbit is an ellipse, and the point where it comes closest to the Sun, the perihelion, slowly turns around the Sun. Newton's gravity explains most of that turning: the other planets tug on Mercury and twist its orbit. By the 1850s astronomers had added up every tug they could and still came up short by a small amount each century. Nobody could find the missing cause until Einstein, in November 1915, computed the effect of curved spacetime and got the missing amount.

An Orbit That Turns

For one planet alone around the Sun, Newton's law of gravity gives a closed ellipse. The planet comes back to exactly the same perihelion every orbit, forever. Turn both effects off in the simulation and you see this: the perihelion line does not move.

Mercury is not alone. Venus, Earth, Jupiter and the rest pull on it, and their pull slightly changes the shape of the force Mercury feels. With that change the orbit no longer closes on itself, and the perihelion creeps forward a little each time around. Relative to the fixed stars, the total turning of Mercury's perihelion is about 575″ (arcseconds) per century[1] (the larger figure sometimes quoted, about 5,600″, includes the slow turning of Earth's own reference direction). An arcsecond is 1/3600 of a degree, so this is about a sixth of a degree per century.

The Leftover Le Verrier Found

In 1859 Urbain Le Verrier, who had predicted Neptune from the wobbles of Uranus, added up the planets' effects on Mercury. His total fell short of what the transits of Mercury across the Sun showed by about 38″ per century[2]. Simon Newcomb redid the work in 1882 and raised the gap to about 43″ per century[3]. Astronomers proposed an unseen planet inside Mercury's orbit (they even named it Vulcan), a ring of dust, and small changes to Newton's law. Searches for Vulcan found nothing, and the other fixes broke the agreement elsewhere in the solar system.

The modern budget, from fitting MESSENGER spacecraft tracking data, splits the turning like this[1]: the Newtonian pull of the other planets accounts for 532.3″ per century, the Sun's slight flattening adds 0.03″, and general relativity adds 42.98″. That last number is the leftover.

Einstein's Correction

In general relativity the Sun's mass curves the space and time around it. Close to the Sun the curvature makes gravity act a little more strongly than Newton's inverse square law says, and the extra effect grows quickly as the planet gets closer. A standard way to write the first correction beyond Newton (the "1PN" order) is an extra radial pull, derived from the orbit equation for the Schwarzschild metric:

$$\vec a = -\frac{GM}{r^3}\left(1 + \frac{3h^2}{c^2 r^2}\right)\vec r$$

where $h$ is the planet's angular momentum per unit mass. That extra term is what the simulation switches on with the GR button. Its effect is to turn the perihelion forward by a fixed angle every orbit:

$$\Delta\varpi = \frac{6\pi G M_\odot}{a(1-e^2)\,c^2}$$

For Mercury, with semi-major axis $a = 0.387$ AU, eccentricity $e = 0.2056$ and period 87.97 days[4], this is about 0.10″ per orbit. Mercury goes around about 415 times per century, so the total is 42.98″ per century. Einstein did this calculation in November 1915 and found 43″, within the 45″ ± 5″ then quoted by astronomers for the gap[5]. It was the one quantitative test available in 1915, and the theory passed it with no adjustable parameter.

Why the Simulation Exaggerates

A tenth of an arcsecond per orbit is far too small to see on a screen. The simulation multiplies both the planets' pull and the GR term by the factor on the slider, so the perihelion turns visibly in seconds. Every readout divides that factor back out and reports real arcseconds per century. The planets are treated as rings of mass spread along their circular orbits, Gauss's way of keeping only their long-term effect. With Mercury's real eccentricity that model gives about 556″ per century, 4 % above the full calculation (it is a planar model with circular planets; the full calculation includes their eccentricities and the tilts of the orbits), and the exaggeration adds a further 1 to 3 % because the amplified rings are no longer a small perturbation. The ring strengths are therefore scaled down by 5 to 7 %, depending on the exaggeration level, so that the planets' part reads the cited 532.3″. That number is a calibration, not a result of the simulation. The GR term is not tuned: the simulation integrates the force above and tracks the turning of the osculating perihelion, and you can compare the sim's own value with the formula.

The dashed orbit that appears in the split view is a second copy of Mercury that feels the planets but not GR. The angle between its perihelion and the real one is the leftover, and it grows at about 43″ per century. At 10,000× both effects are amplified so much that they are no longer small perturbations, and a second-order cross term between them appears: the leftover the sim shows comes out about 2.5″ low, and the GR-only value about 0.2″ high. Both deficits shrink in proportion to the exaggeration; at 1,000× the leftover is within 0.3″ of the formula.

Key Takeaway
  • Alone with the Sun, a Newtonian planet repeats the same ellipse forever; the other planets make Mercury's perihelion turn by about 532″ per century (cited).
  • The turning seen in Mercury's motion is about 43″ per century more than the planets explain. General relativity's 1PN term predicts 42.98″.
  • Einstein's 1915 result needed no adjustable parameters; the light-bending measurement of 1919 followed.

$$\Delta\varpi_{\text{GR}} = \frac{6\pi G M_\odot}{a(1-e^2)\,c^2}$$

For Mercury: about 0.1035″ per orbit, or 42.98″ per century.
