The Doppler Effect

Why an ambulance sounds different as it passes

1842 · Christian Doppler

In 1842 Christian Doppler proposed, in a lecture in Prague, that a moving source of waves is heard or seen at a shifted frequency, and he applied it, wrongly, to the colours of double stars. The effect itself was right. For sound it was confirmed in 1845 by Buys Ballot with musicians on a moving train: when a source moves, the wavefronts it emits bunch up in front and spread apart behind. That bunching is a frequency shift — and it became the ruler astronomers use to measure the expansion of the universe.

The Doppler formula

When a source moves toward an observer at speed \(v_s\) and waves travel at \(v_w\), the observed frequency is:

$$f_\text{obs} = f_0 \cdot \frac{v_w}{v_w \mp v_s}$$

The minus sign applies when the source approaches (higher frequency); the plus sign when it recedes (lower frequency). For light and slow sources (\(v_s \ll c\)):

$$\frac{\Delta f}{f_0} \approx \frac{v_s}{c}$$

This is the formula Hubble used in 1929 with Slipher's velocities to show that almost every galaxy is receding — and the further away it is, the faster it moves. The Doppler effect, proposed for starlight in a Prague lecture hall and first checked with trumpets on a train, turned out to measure the size and fate of the cosmos.

Key Concept
  • A source moving toward you compresses its wavefronts — observed frequency rises. Moving away stretches them — frequency drops. The formula is \(f = f_0 \, v_w/(v_w \mp v_s)\). For nearby galaxies the redshift reads as a Doppler shift, cz ≈ v; for distant ones it is better described as the stretching of light by the expansion of space, 1 + z = a_now/a_then, which is not a speed.
