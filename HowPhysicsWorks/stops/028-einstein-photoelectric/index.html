Einstein and the Photoelectric Effect

Light comes in quanta

1905 · Albert Einstein

The question

Why does brighter light below a threshold frequency free no electrons from sodium, and what sets the energy of the electrons it does free?

Einstein explained why shining light on metal ejects electrons — but only if the light frequency is high enough. This won him the 1921 Nobel Prize and was the strongest early argument that light comes in quanta.

The Classical Prediction (Wrong)

Classical wave theory predicted that if you shone bright enough light on metal, eventually enough energy would accumulate to eject electrons. The brighter the light, the more energy deposited — so crank up the intensity long enough and electrons would eventually escape. Try it: drag the intensity slider to maximum while the frequency slider is below the threshold. Nothing happens. No electrons, no matter how bright the light. Philipp Lenard had already found, in 1902, that brighter light gives more electrons but not faster ones, and that their maximum energy depends only on the light's colour. Classical theory had no answer.[2]

Einstein's Insight: Photons

In 1905 Einstein proposed that light comes in discrete packets — photons — each carrying energy E = hf. Only a photon of high enough frequency (enough energy per packet) can knock an electron loose. More photons means more intensity, but each photon still carries the same energy. Intensity controls the rate of ejection; frequency controls whether ejection is possible at all. Einstein's picture also explains why the electrons leave at once, even in faint light: one photon is absorbed whole, so there is nothing to accumulate.[1]

The Work Function

Each metal has a characteristic work function φ — the minimum energy needed to free an electron from the surface. Any energy beyond φ becomes kinetic energy of the ejected electron: KE = hf − φ. This linear relationship between KE and frequency (not intensity) was Einstein's key prediction, verified experimentally by Millikan in 1916, who measured h = 6.57 × 10−34 J·s from the slope.[3] In the simulation, the metal is sodium with φ ≈ 2.3 eV, so the threshold photon energy is 2.3 eV.

What the Simulation Leaves Out

The frequency slider has no units: across its range the photon energy hf runs from 1.38 to 4.60 eV against a fixed φ = 2.30 eV, and the photon colour shades from red to violet as a cue, not the true colour of each energy. Intensity sets how many photons are in flight at once, from 1 to 9, and above threshold every photon that reaches the plate frees one electron, with at most 20 shown. Every electron leaves with the same, maximum kinetic energy hf − φ; in a real metal most leave with less. Electron speeds and their slight downward curve are drawn for display, and there is no collector or stopping voltage.

Key Equation

$$KE = hf - \varphi$$

Above the threshold frequency \(f_0 = \varphi/h\), kinetic energy increases linearly with \(f\), not with intensity.
