Thales and Natural Causes

What if the universe runs on rules, not gods?

~600 BCE · Thales of Miletus

Before Thales, the Greeks explained earthquakes as Poseidon shaking the ground, lightning as Zeus throwing bolts, and eclipses as divine omens. These explanations worked — they gave frightened people a story — but they made no predictions you could test.

Thales made one move that changed everything: he asked whether nature has patterns that repeat regardless of what any god wants. He proposed that earthquakes happen because the Earth floats on water, and water disturbs it. He was wrong about the mechanism. But the question itself — "what natural process causes this?" — is the founding question of all science.

Below is the measurement later writers credit to him, and you can repeat it. The Sun's rays arrive parallel, so a stick and a pyramid standing in the same sunlight cast shadows in the same proportion as their heights. Measure both shadows and you know a height you cannot climb.

Two saros series of total solar eclipses. Each eclipse in a series comes 6,585 or 6,586 calendar days after the one before (18 years and 10, 11 or 12 days, depending on leap years), because 223 lunar months last 6,585.32 days. That repeat is the kind of pattern a careful record of dates can reveal. The extra third of a day matters: the Earth has turned about a third of a turn further (0.32 × 360° ≈ 115°), so in these two series each eclipse is greatest between 106° and 127° of longitude further west than the one before. A cycle like this can say that an eclipse may occur; it cannot say where it will be seen.

Why This Moment Matters

Thales lived in Miletus, a trading city on the coast of present-day Turkey. He had contact with Babylonian astronomers and Egyptian geometers — people who had already noticed that the sky follows patterns. Thales absorbed this and drew the radical conclusion: if the sky has patterns, so does everything else.

Herodotus (Histories I.74) says the Medes and the Lydians were fighting when "day became night", that Thales had foretold the year, and that the two kings then made peace, brokered by Cilician and Babylonian envoys. The eclipse of 28 May 585 BCE is real and can be computed today; whether Thales predicted it is doubted by most historians of astronomy, because no Babylonian or Greek method of the time could predict where a solar eclipse would be visible.

His specific theories about what things are made of (he thought water was the fundamental substance) turned out to be wrong. But the method — look for a natural cause, form a prediction, check it against reality — became the engine of 2,500 years of discovery.

How the Shadow Measurement Works

Two later writers tell the story. Diogenes Laertius quotes Hieronymus: Thales measured the pyramids by their shadow, waiting for the hour when a person's shadow is as long as the person is tall[4]. At that moment the Sun stands 45° high and every shadow equals its object's height, which is why the simulation starts at 45°. Plutarch gives the general version: Thales set a staff upright at the end of the pyramid's shadow, and the two triangles made by the Sun's rays showed that the pyramid is to the staff as shadow is to shadow[4]. That proportion works at any altitude:

$$S_\text{stick} = \frac{h}{\tan\alpha}, \quad S_\text{pyramid} = \frac{H}{\tan\alpha} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{H}{h} = \frac{S_\text{pyramid}}{S_\text{stick}}$$

Both shadows are a height divided by \(\tan\alpha\), where \(\alpha\) is the Sun's altitude, so \(\alpha\) cancels and you never need to know it. There is a catch the stick does not have. The pyramid's height is measured at its apex, which stands above the centre of the base, so its shadow must be measured from that centre too. Half the base, 115.2 m (the base side is 230.3 m[5]), lies hidden under the pyramid; you add it to the visible shadow. Forget it and your estimate comes out far too low.

The simulation draws the pyramid at its original height, 146.6 m[5], and the stick at 2.00 m, an illustrative choice; the sources do not give the staff's length. The stick is drawn magnified in its own panel so you can read its shadow. Above 51.8°, the slope of the faces[5], the whole shadow falls on the pyramid itself and nothing reaches past the base: try it with the slider.

What the Simulation Leaves Out

The side view assumes the Sun is square to one face, as at noon, when the shadow points due north. At Giza's latitude (30° N) the noon Sun is low enough for a shadow to reach past the base only from about mid-October to the end of February (worked out here from the Sun's yearly path, not taken from a source); at other times of day the method still works, but the distance from the centre has to be measured along a slanting shadow. The simulation also draws a sharp shadow. The real Sun is a disc half a degree wide, so the tip of the pyramid's shadow is blurred over two to three metres at 45° (the Sun's half-degree width, spread over the 207 m from apex to tip and projected onto the ground), which limits how well anyone could have read it.

Key Takeaway
  • Thales is the earliest Greek thinker we know of who explained natural events (earthquakes, the Nile flood, the nature of matter) by natural causes. Our knowledge of him is second-hand: Aristotle, Herodotus and later writers; nothing he wrote survives.
  • The pyramid measurement attributed to him uses a natural regularity, that sunlight travels in parallel straight lines, to find a height nobody could reach directly.
  • He was wrong about the specifics (water as the fundamental substance) but right about the approach: nature has patterns that repeat and can be predicted.
  • Herodotus says he foretold the year of the eclipse of 28 May 585 BCE. Historians doubt he could have: no method known in his time predicts a solar eclipse for a given place, and the story may be a later attribution. It is a legend worth telling as a legend.
