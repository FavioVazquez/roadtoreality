Newton's Laws of Motion

F = ma. Three laws that ran physics for 200 years, and still run it at everyday speeds.

1687 · Isaac Newton

The question

If nothing pushes a moving block, does it stop? And how much does the same push speed up a heavier block?

In 1687, Newton published the Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica, the Principia — arguably the most important scientific book ever written. In it, he laid out three laws that describe how every object in the universe responds to forces.

Push the block below. Watch how force, mass, and acceleration relate. Notice that when you stop pushing, the block doesn't stop — it keeps moving (First Law). Heavier blocks accelerate less for the same force (Second Law). And the block pushes back against the wall with equal force (Third Law).

Set force and mass, then apply. Watch F = ma in action.

What to try

  1. Set Surface friction to None, press Apply Force, then Release Force: the v(t) trace goes flat and the block coasts at constant speed until it reaches the wall.
  2. With friction at None and 10 N applied, change Mass from 5 kg to 10 kg while the force is on: the a readout drops from 2.00 to 1.00 m/s², half the acceleration for twice the mass.
  3. Set friction to High, mass to 20 kg and Applied force to 30 N: F_net reads 0 and the block stays put, because friction can supply up to 0.18 × 20 kg × 9.8 m/s² ≈ 35 N. At 40 N it starts to slide.
  4. While the force is on, watch the two arrows at the hand: the push on the block and the push back on the hand are always equal and opposite.

The Three Laws

First Law (Inertia): An object at rest stays at rest; an object in motion stays in motion, unless acted on by an external force. Aristotle's physics needed a mover to keep anything moving. Galileo saw that motion on a level surface would persist without one; Descartes stated the law in full in 1644; Newton made it the first of his three and credited Galileo.

Second Law (in modern form F = ma; Newton wrote it as the change of motion being proportional to the impressed force): The acceleration of an object is proportional to the net force applied and inversely proportional to its mass. Double the force, double the acceleration. Double the mass, halve the acceleration.

Third Law (Action-Reaction): Every force has an equal and opposite reaction. When you push a wall, the wall pushes back with the same force. When a rocket expels gas backward, the gas pushes the rocket forward.

These three laws — combined with his law of gravitation — gave physics a unified mathematical framework that explained everything from falling apples to planetary orbits.

What the Simulation Leaves Out

Friction uses one coefficient for starting and for sliding (0, 0.03, 0.08 and 0.18 for None to High) with g = 9.8 m/s², and there is no air drag. When the block hits the wall it rebounds at half its speed; that is a choice made for the display, not something Newton's laws fix. The block and the wall are drawn at a fixed size, not to scale.

Key Takeaway
  • Objects don't need a force to keep moving — they need a force to change their motion (1st Law).
  • F = ma: acceleration is proportional to force and inversely proportional to mass (2nd Law).
  • Forces always come in equal and opposite pairs — you can never push without being pushed back (3rd Law).
