Euler and Rigid Body Rotation

Spinning things: why the shape of a body, not just its mass, sets how it turns

1750–1765 · Leonhard Euler

Newton's second law, in the form F = ma we all learn, was first written down by Euler in 1750, and Euler then did the same for rotation. A force applied off-center creates a torque, and that torque produces angular acceleration — but how much depends not on mass alone, but on how that mass is distributed around the axis of rotation.

The three shapes below — rod, disk, and ring — have identical mass, the same outer size, and the same torque applied. Watch their angular velocities diverge. The rod spins up fastest, because most of its mass sits near the axis; the ring slowest, because all of its mass sits at the rim. Torque and angular acceleration

The rotational analogue of Newton's second law is:

$$\tau = I \alpha$$

where \(\tau\) is torque (N·m), \(I\) is moment of inertia (kg·m²), and \(\alpha\) is angular acceleration (rad/s²). The shape of the object determines \(I\).

Moments of inertia

For the three shapes in the simulation, each rotating about an axis through its centre, with the rod's length equal to the disk's diameter (L = 2r):

$$I_{\text{rod}} = \tfrac{1}{12}mL^2 \qquad I_{\text{disk}} = \tfrac{1}{2}mr^2 \qquad I_{\text{ring}} = mr^2$$

Given the same torque, the rod accelerates fastest (\(I = mr^2/3\) is smallest), the ring slowest (\(I = mr^2\) is largest), the disk in between (\(I = mr^2/2\)). The angular velocity at time \(t\) is:

$$\omega(t) = \alpha \, t = \frac{\tau}{I} \, t$$

The graph on the right shows three diverging straight lines — one per shape — confirming that angular velocity rises linearly at a rate inversely proportional to \(I\).

Euler's equations for 3D rotation

Between 1750 (his 'new principle of mechanics') and 1765 (Theoria motus corporum solidorum), Euler extended this to a set of equations relating the angular velocity components \(\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3\) along the principal axes of a body to the applied torques. These equations govern gyroscope precession, spacecraft attitude control, and the wobbly path of a thrown frisbee.

Key Equation
  • \(\tau = I\alpha\) — the rotational analogue of Newton's second law. A body with more mass spread far from the axis (high \(I\)) is harder to spin. This is why figure skaters pull in their arms to speed up (angular momentum \(L = I\omega\) is conserved, so a smaller \(I\) means a larger \(\omega\)), and why hollow wheels roll differently than solid ones.
