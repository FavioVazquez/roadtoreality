Rutherford and the Atomic Nucleus

The atom is mostly empty space

1911 · Ernest Rutherford

Rutherford fired alpha particles at gold foil. Most passed straight through — but a few bounced directly back. The atom has a tiny, dense nucleus at its center.

The Plum Pudding Model (Wrong)

In 1909, the prevailing model of the atom — Thomson's "plum pudding" (his own 1910 calculation gave only small-angle multiple scattering, which is why the large angles mattered) — had positive charge spread uniformly throughout the atom, with electrons embedded like raisins in dough. Under this model, alpha particles should barely deflect when passing through gold foil. The positive charge would be too diffuse to cause significant scattering.

The Unexpected Result

Most alpha particles passed almost straight through, which fits an atom that is mostly empty. But Geiger and Marsden found in 1909 that about 1 in 8,000, from a platinum sheet, came back at more than 90 degrees, and in 1913 they measured the full angular distribution from gold foil. Rutherford recalled, decades later, that it was "almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you." No diffuse charge distribution could cause that.

The simulation draws the nucleus thousands of times too large relative to the atom, so that a backscatter happens every few seconds instead of once in eight thousand; the angle for a given aim follows Rutherford's formula exactly.

The Nuclear Model

The only way to explain the backscattering was if all the positive charge — and nearly all the mass — was concentrated in an impossibly tiny region at the center. Rutherford called it the "central charge" in 1911 and the nucleus from 1912. It is tens of thousands of times smaller than the atom: for gold, about 7 femtometres against an atomic radius of 0.14 nanometres. Rutherford's own 1911 estimate from the fastest alphas available was that the central charge sits inside about 3 × 10⁻¹⁴ m. The electrons orbit this nucleus in the vast empty space around it.

Rutherford Scattering Formula

$$\cot\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{2b \, E_k}{k Z_1 Z_2 e^2}$$

Larger $b$ (impact parameter) = smaller deflection angle $\theta$. A head-on collision ($b = 0$) gives backscatter ($\theta = 180°$). Here $k = 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$, $E_k$ is the alpha's kinetic energy, $Z_1 = 2$ and $Z_2 = Z$.
