Bernoulli's Principle

Why airplanes fly and curveballs curve

1738 · Daniel Bernoulli

In 1738, Daniel Bernoulli published Hydrodynamica and made a startling observation: when a fluid is forced through a narrow section of pipe, it speeds up — and as it speeds up, its pressure drops. The two effects are inseparable. Energy is conserved, but it trades between kinetic and pressure form.

The continuity equation

Before Bernoulli, fluids already obeyed one simple rule: what flows in must flow out. For an incompressible fluid, the product of cross-sectional area and velocity is constant:

$$A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2$$

A narrower throat means a higher velocity. That is the continuity equation — purely geometric, no energy required.

Where pressure enters

Bernoulli's insight (1738) was that a fluid's pressure falls where it speeds up, which he derived from the conservation of vis viva; the equation in its modern form is Euler's, from his 1755 memoir on fluid motion. The total per unit volume — the pressure term, kinetic energy and gravitational potential — stays constant:

$$P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{const}$$

At constant height this simplifies to \(P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 = \text{const}\). If \(v\) rises at the throat (continuity demands it), \(P\) must fall by exactly \(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho(v_2^2 - v_1^2)\). That pressure difference is what a venturi meter reads, and it is half of the story of a wing: the wing turns the air downward, the flow over the top is faster, and Bernoulli links that speed to the lower pressure that holds the aircraft up.

Real applications

Carburetors, atomizers, venturi flowmeters, the pressure side of a wing's lift — all follow the same exchange. Faster fluid, lower pressure. The principle is deceptively simple and extraordinarily useful.

Key Equation
  • \(P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 = \text{const}\) — along a streamline at constant height. Where fluid speeds up, pressure drops by \(\Delta P = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho(v_2^2 - v_1^2)\). Along a streamline, for steady flow of an incompressible fluid with no friction.
