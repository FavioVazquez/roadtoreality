Joule and Conservation of Energy

Heat is just motion in disguise

1843–1850 · James Prescott Joule

The question

When a falling weight turns a paddle in water, where does its potential energy go, and does the total ever change?

James Prescott Joule was a brewer who became one of the most precise experimentalists in history. He showed that mechanical work and heat are interchangeable — that they are both forms of the same thing, energy. His experiments of 1843–1850, ending with the famous paddle-wheel, measured the conversion ratio with extraordinary accuracy, and in doing so gave the first law of thermodynamics its experimental foundation (Mayer had argued for the equivalence in 1842; Helmholtz generalised it in 1847): energy is conserved across all its forms.

The Experiment

Joule suspended a known mass from a cord wound around a paddle-wheel axle. When the mass fell, it rotated the paddle through water. The temperature rise in the water was measured with extraordinary precision — a few hundredths of a degree per drop. By letting the weights fall twenty times per run and averaging dozens of runs, Joule found that 772.7 foot-pounds of work warm a pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit, which in today's units is 4.16 joules per calorie, within 0.7 % of the modern value (4.186 J). The energy bars show the first drop: the kinetic energy of the slowly falling weight is only 0.09 J of the 29.4 J at the defaults.

Why It Was Controversial

In 1843, most physicists believed in caloric — a weightless fluid that flowed from hot bodies to cold ones. Joule's result implied that heat is not a substance but a mode of motion. The temperature rise is tiny (drop a 2 kg weight from 1.5 m and you heat 1 kg of water by just 0.007°C), but it is perfectly reproducible — which is the whole point.

What the Simulation Leaves Out

The weight always takes 5 s to fall, whatever the height, and moves at constant speed from the first instant, so its kinetic energy is ½m(h/5 s)² with no speed-up phase. Every joule of potential energy lost is booked as heat in the water, with g = 9.8 m/s²: nothing goes into the pulley, the cord or the floor, and the small kinetic energy left at the bottom is added to the heat. The sim does not compute a temperature; the water’s colour shows the fraction of energy converted, not a thermometer reading.

Key Concepts
  • Energy is conserved — it changes form, but the total never changes. Joule's measurements showed that mechanical work and heat are interchangeable.
  • $$E_{\text{total}} = mgh = \underbrace{mg(h-y)}_{\text{PE}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2}_{\text{KE}} + \underbrace{Q}_{\text{heat}}$$
  • Joule's mechanical equivalent of heat (1850): 772.7 ft·lbf per Btu = 4.16 J per cal; modern value 4.186 J
  • This result unified mechanics and thermodynamics, displacing the caloric theory of heat. It became the foundation of the first law of thermodynamics.
