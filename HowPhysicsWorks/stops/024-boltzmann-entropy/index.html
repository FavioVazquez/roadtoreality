Boltzmann and Entropy

Why the universe always gets messier

1877 · Ludwig Boltzmann

The question

Why does a gas spread out once the wall is removed and never gather back, and how does temperature shift its speeds?

In 1877, Ludwig Boltzmann gave entropy a precise definition: it counts the number of microscopic arrangements (microstates) \(\Omega\) that produce the same macroscopic appearance. Because high-entropy states have astronomically more microstates than low-entropy ones, a system evolving randomly will almost certainly move toward disorder — not because nature "wants" disorder, but because there are so many more ways to be disordered. (Boltzmann wrote the relation as a proportionality; the equation with the constant k, S = k log W, is Planck's 1900 form, and that is what is carved on Boltzmann's gravestone in Vienna.)

What to try

  1. Press Remove Wall: the S / S_max meter climbs from 0% as particles cross, then hovers in the 90s and stays there. It reads 100% only when exactly 25 of the 50 are on each side, about 1.3 × 10¹⁴ arrangements against one for all on the left.
  2. Drag Temperature from T = 1.0 (relative) to T = 3.0: the particles speed up, and the bars and the yellow MB curve move to higher speeds and spread out.
  3. Drag Temperature down to T = 0.2: the particles slow and the distribution bunches up at the low-speed end of the axis.

The Maxwell-Boltzmann speed distribution

In a gas at temperature \(T\), particle speeds are not all equal — they follow a statistical distribution. In two dimensions, the probability density is:

$$f(v) = \frac{m}{k_B T}\, v\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{mv^2}{2k_B T}\right)$$

The peak shifts to higher speeds as temperature rises, and the distribution broadens. The yellow curve in the left panel shows this theoretical prediction.

Boltzmann entropy

Entropy \(S\) is proportional to the logarithm of the number of microstates \(\Omega\):

$$S = k_B \ln \Omega$$

When the right-panel partition is removed, the gas expands into twice the volume. The number of accessible microstates doubles for each particle, so \(\Omega \to 2^N \Omega_0\) and entropy increases by \(\Delta S = N k_B \ln 2\). The entropy meter shows \(S/S_\text{max}\) for the coarse macrostate "n of the N particles are on the right", S = k ln [N!/(n!(N−n)!)]: zero with all on one side, maximum at an even split. With 50 particles the even split has 1.3 × 1014 arrangements and the starting state one.

What the Simulation Leaves Out

  • Both gases are two-dimensional hard disks of equal mass (60 on the left, 50 on the right) with perfectly elastic collisions; speeds are in pixels per frame and T is a relative setting with no kB.
  • The left gas starts with speeds spread evenly between 0.5 and 2 times the base speed and reaches the Maxwell–Boltzmann shape through collisions; the yellow curve uses the spread measured from the particles’ current speeds, and the bars are averaged over about a second, so they lag a slider change.
  • The right gas does not respond to the Temperature slider.
  • The meter counts only how many particles are on the right, the coarse macrostate on the page; positions within each half and speeds are not counted.
Key Concept
  • Boltzmann's equation \(S = k_B \ln \Omega\) links the macroscopic concept of entropy to the count of microscopic arrangements. The second law of thermodynamics — the entropy of an isolated system does not decrease, except for fluctuations that become negligible for large N — is really a statement about probability: disordered states are overwhelmingly more likely than ordered ones.
  • The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution \(f(v) \propto v \exp(-mv^2/2k_BT)\) describes how particle speeds are distributed in thermal equilibrium at temperature \(T\).
