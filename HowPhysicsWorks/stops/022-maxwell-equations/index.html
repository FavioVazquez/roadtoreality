Maxwell's Equations

Four equations that contain all of classical electromagnetism

1865 · James Clerk Maxwell

The question

Where do electric field lines start and end, and how do oscillating E and B fields line up as a wave travels?

James Clerk Maxwell looked at all of the known laws of electricity and magnetism — Gauss, Faraday, Ampère — and realized they were inconsistent. By adding one term (the "displacement current") he made them consistent, and in doing so predicted that oscillating electric and magnetic fields would sustain each other in a self-propagating wave. He calculated the speed from purely electrical measurements: 310,740 km/s, against Fizeau's 314,858 km/s for light, a match to about 1 % with no free parameter. Light is an electromagnetic wave.

What to try

  1. Drag the +q or −q charge in the left panel: the 16 lines from +q are traced again at once. Follow each one to see whether it ends on −q or runs off the edge of the panel.
  2. Raise Wave frequency from 1.0 to 2.0: twice as many wavelengths fit across the right panel and the readout reads freq: 2.0× (wave speed stays c); the crests still move across at the same speed.
  3. Move Wave amplitude from 0.60 to 1.00: the E arrows and the B curve grow together. Where the blue E arrows shrink to nothing the orange B markers vanish too, and where E points up B is a dot (out of the screen): they rise and fall in phase.

What You Are Seeing

Left panel — the static field, as a reminder. Gauss's law says the number of lines leaving +q equals the number arriving at −q if every line is followed to its end. The panel cuts lines off at its edge, so only the ones that curve round to −q are seen to arrive; the rest are drawn leaving. The field lines (traced by following the electric field vector step by step) originate on the positive charge and terminate on the negative charge. Drag either charge to see the field lines recompute in real time.

Right panel — EM wave. Blue vertical arrows show the electric field (E) oscillating perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Orange markers (dots = out of screen, crosses = into screen) show the magnetic field (B) perpendicular to both E and the propagation direction. E and B rise and fall together, in phase, with E × B pointing along the direction of travel. Together, E and B sustain each other — this is what Maxwell predicted: light.

What the Simulation Leaves Out

The left panel traces 16 lines from +q only, in a flat plane, and stops each line at the panel edge, so lines that head outward are cut off instead of curving back to −q. The two panels are separate pictures: the charges on the left are static and do not radiate, and the wave on the right is a drawn plane wave, not computed from them. Frequency and amplitude are relative display settings, the on-screen wave speed stands for c without being to scale, and B is drawn at a fixed fraction of E’s length in a flattened perspective.

Maxwell's Four Equations
  • The four compact equations below are Heaviside's 1880s form of Maxwell's twenty.
  • Gauss's Law (electric): electric field lines originate on positive charges and terminate on negative charges.
  • $$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}$$
  • Gauss's Law (magnetic): there are no magnetic monopoles — field lines always form closed loops.
  • $$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$$
  • Faraday's Law: a changing magnetic field creates an electric field.
  • $$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$$
  • Ampère-Maxwell Law: a changing electric field (or current) creates a magnetic field.
  • $$\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \mathbf{J} + \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$$
  • Together, these four equations predict that oscillating electric and magnetic fields sustain each other — forming a self-propagating wave at speed \(c = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}} \approx 3 \times 10^8\) m/s.
