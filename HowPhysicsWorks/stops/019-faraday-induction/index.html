Faraday's Electromagnetic Induction

Move a magnet through a coil — electricity appears

1831 · Michael Faraday

The question

Why does the galvanometer needle move only while the magnet moves, and why does it flip as the magnet passes through the coil?

Faraday — a bookbinder's apprentice with almost no formal education — discovered that a changing magnetic field creates an electric current. Move the magnet through the coil below and watch the galvanometer needle respond to the rate of change of flux, not to flux itself. Pull the magnet back out and the needle flips direction — Lenz's law in action.

What to try

  1. With the simulation running, drag Magnet position to about 10% (this stops the swing), then toward the coil, and stop: while the magnet moves the readout says [flux rising] and the needle swings; the moment it stops, the needle returns to the centre.
  2. Keep dragging the same way past about 53%, where the magnet's centre crosses the coil's centre: the needle flips, the readout changes to [flux falling], and the yellow N and S marks on the coil swap.
  3. Drag the same distance slowly, then quickly: the faster move gives the larger ℰ number and the wider needle swing.
  4. Press Auto Oscillate (it reads Stop Oscillation while the magnet swings): the needle reverses four times per cycle, at each end of the swing and each time the magnet's centre crosses the coil.

What's happening

The galvanometer needle is driven by the time-derivative of magnetic flux through the coil — dΦ/dt — not by the flux itself. When the magnet sits still, no current flows. Only movement creates induction. And when the magnet reverses direction, the induced current reverses too (Lenz's law). The needle also reverses once during every pass, at the moment the magnet's centre crosses the coil, because the flux stops rising and starts falling. The yellow dots drift along the leads in the direction of the induced current; the N and S marks show which face of the coil has become which pole, always the one that opposes the magnet's motion (an approaching N pole meets a coil face that has become N). The coil in the picture has five turns.

What the Simulation Leaves Out

The flux is a smooth bell-shaped function of the distance between the magnet's centre and the coil, not a field computed from a real bar magnet, and the ℰ readout is in arbitrary units. The rate of change is taken between successive frames, assumed 0.016 s apart, so the needle only reads correctly while the simulation is running. The induced current does not push back on the magnet, the coil has no resistance or self-inductance, and the needle stops at 60°. The moving dots and the induced N and S marks are drawn only when ℰ is large enough to matter.

Key Concepts
  • A changing magnetic flux through a coil induces an electromotive force (EMF). The magnitude depends on how fast the flux changes — not on the flux itself.
  • $$\mathcal{E} = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$
  • This is for a coil of N turns (five in the picture). Faraday stated the law in words and pictures of lines of force; the equation is Maxwell's (1855–1861).
  • Lenz's law (Heinrich Lenz, 1834): the induced current opposes the change that caused it. Move the magnet in → current one way; pull it out → current reverses.
  • $$\Phi_B = \int \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A}$$
  • Every generator, transformer, and induction motor in the world runs on this principle.
