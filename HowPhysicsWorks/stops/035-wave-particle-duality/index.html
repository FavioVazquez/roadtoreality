Wave-Particle Duality

Electrons are waves too

1924 · Louis de Broglie

Light behaves as a wave when it passes two slits and as a stream of particles when it knocks electrons out of a metal. In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that matter has a wavelength too, $\lambda = h/p$, where $h$ is Planck's constant and $p$ the momentum[1]. Electrons were seen to diffract in 1927[3][4].

The simulation sends electrons through two slits one at a time. Each one is detected as a single dot. Where the dots pile up is set by the wave.

Two Pictures of Light

In 1803 Thomas Young split a narrow sunbeam around a thin card and saw bright and dark bands where the two parts overlapped, the signature of interfering waves[5]; he later described the same effect with two pinholes. In 1905 Einstein argued that light also carries energy in separate quanta of size $hf$, which explained how light ejects electrons from metals[2]. Neither picture alone covers everything light does.

De Broglie's Proposal

In 1923–24, in short notes and then his thesis, de Broglie suggested the same double nature for matter, assigning a particle of momentum $p$ the wavelength[11][1]

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$

Everyday objects have wavelengths far too small to notice. Electrons are light enough for the wavelength to matter. For an electron accelerated through a voltage the momentum follows from its kinetic energy $E$ and rest energy $mc^2$:

$$p\,c = \sqrt{E^2 + 2E\,mc^2}$$

At 50 keV this gives a wavelength of about 5.4 picometres, using the CODATA values of $h$, $m_e$ and $c$[8]. That is about a hundred thousand times shorter than visible light.

In 1927 Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer found that 54 eV electrons scattered from a nickel crystal came off strongly at angles that matched de Broglie's wavelength[3]. The same year George Paget Thomson and Alexander Reid saw diffraction rings from electrons passed through thin films[4]. These diffraction experiments, not the double slit, were what confirmed de Broglie's idea; the electron double slit came decades later.

One Electron at a Time

Claus Jönsson made an electron version of Young's experiment in 1961, with slits cut into a metal foil[6]. In 1989 Akira Tonomura and colleagues sent electrons through an electron biprism, which plays the part of two slits, at a rate so low that there was usually only one electron in the apparatus at a time[7]. Each electron arrived as a single spot on the detector. As thousands of spots accumulated they formed interference fringes.

That is what the simulation shows. Each dot lands at a random place. The chance of landing at position $y$ on the screen is set by the wave passing both slits. For slits of width $a$, separation $d$ and a screen at distance $D$, the textbook far-field pattern is[9]

$$P(y) \propto \cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi d y}{\lambda D}\right)\,\mathrm{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{\pi a y}{\lambda D}\right)$$

with bright fringes $\lambda D/d$ apart. A single dot says almost nothing about the pattern; the fringes only appear in the statistics.

Blocking a Slit, or Asking Which Slit

Block one slit and the fringes vanish: the dots spread over the wide, smooth pattern of a single slit. In 2013 Roger Bach and colleagues did this with a movable mask in front of an electron double slit and recorded the pattern as one slit, then both, were opened[12]. The "Block one slit" button shows this case.

If the apparatus records which slit each electron went through, the fringes disappear and the dots spread over the smooth pattern of two single slits added together[9]. The "Detect which slit" button switches to that case. The quantum description does not say the electron is "really" a wave or "really" a particle. It gives probabilities, and those probabilities add like waves when the two paths cannot be told apart.

The same interference has since been seen with neutrons, atoms and molecules; in 1999 it was reported for C60 molecules of 60 carbon atoms[10].

What the Simulation Assumes

The geometry is illustrative and does not reproduce any particular experiment: slits 0.2 µm wide, a screen 1 m away, and a screen window 80 µm across, and the far-field formula is used throughout even at the widest slit separation, where it is only approximate. Real electron experiments use electron optics to magnify a much finer pattern. The landing points are random draws from $P(y)$, computed from the formula, not data. The arrival rate starts slow so single dots are visible, then speeds up so the pattern builds in seconds; this rate is a display choice.

Key Takeaway
  • De Broglie (1924) gave matter a wavelength $\lambda = h/p$; about 5.4 pm for a 50 keV electron. Electron diffraction confirmed it in 1927 (Davisson and Germer; Thomson and Reid).
  • Electrons sent one at a time each land as a single dot, but the dots build up an interference pattern (Tonomura et al. 1989, cited).
  • Blocking one slit, or recording which slit each electron used, removes the fringes.

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} \qquad \Delta y_{\text{fringe}} = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$$
