The Pauli Exclusion Principle

Why matter is solid

1925 · Wolfgang Pauli

In 1925 Wolfgang Pauli proposed that no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of quantum numbers[1]. That one rule fixes how electrons stack up in atoms, which shapes the periodic table, and it is why ordinary matter does not collapse.

One Electron per State

By 1925 the electron states of an atom were labelled with quantum numbers, but the labels did not explain why the shells close where they do. Pauli's answer had two parts[1]. Each electron needs a fourth quantum number, one that takes only two values. And no two electrons in an atom may have all four the same. Later in 1925 George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit identified the two-valued number as the electron's spin[2].

The four numbers are the shell $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$; the orbital shape $l = 0, \dots, n-1$ (written s, p, d, f); its orientation $m_l = -l, \dots, +l$; and the spin $m_s = \pm\tfrac12$. Counting them gives the capacity of each shell[8]:

$$\sum_{l=0}^{n-1} 2(2l+1) = 2n^2$$

That is 2, 8, 18 and 32 for the first four shells. In the simulation each box is one $(n, l, m_l)$ state with room for one spin-up and one spin-down electron.

Building the Periodic Table

Add electrons one at a time to the lowest free state and the periodic table appears. The order of the subshells, 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, is the empirical rule for many-electron atoms named after Erwin Madelung; 4s fills before 3d because in potassium and calcium it lies slightly lower; in the transition metals the two are close, which is why the rule has exceptions[8]. The outermost s and p subshells are both full at 2, 10, 18 and 36 electrons: helium, neon, argon and krypton, the noble gases. Only helium and neon have a whole shell full; from argon on, the d states of the same $n$ fill later. The Madelung rule is not exact. Chromium and copper move one 4s electron into 3d, and the simulation says so when you reach them.

Switch the exclusion off and every electron drops into 1s. There would be no shells, no chemistry of the kind we know, and no periodic table.

Why the Rule Holds

Electrons are identical, and quantum mechanics requires the wavefunction of identical electrons to change sign when any two are swapped. Put two in the same state and swapping them changes nothing, so the wavefunction must equal its own negative: it is zero. Particles that obey this are called fermions; Pauli's later spin–statistics theorem of 1940 ties the rule to half-integer spin[5][8].

Why Matter Is Solid

Squeeze electrons into a smaller space and the exclusion principle forces them into states of higher momentum, because the low ones are already taken. The result is a pressure that does not need heat, called degeneracy pressure. For electrons that are slow compared with light it grows as the density to the power 5/3[6]:

$$P = \frac{(3\pi^2)^{2/3}}{5}\,\frac{\hbar^2}{m_e}\,n_e^{5/3}$$

In 1967 Freeman Dyson and Andrew Lenard gave a rigorous proof that bulk matter is stable[3]; Dyson's companion paper had shown that without the exclusion principle matter would collapse[5].

The Limit of a White Dwarf

A white dwarf is a star held up against gravity by electron degeneracy pressure. Squeeze harder and the electrons approach the speed of light. The pressure then grows only as $n_e^{4/3}$, and above a certain mass gravity wins[6]. The star view of the simulation plots the full formula for a cold electron gas and marks the two limits. In 1931 Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar computed the maximum mass, in a paper titled "The Maximum Mass of Ideal White Dwarfs"[4]; he assumed 2.5 nucleons per electron, the composition then believed likely[4]. The textbook result is

$$M_{\text{Ch}} = \frac{\sqrt{3\pi}}{2}\,\omega_3\left(\frac{\hbar c}{G}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{(\mu_e m_u)^2}$$

with $\omega_3 \approx 2.018$ from the solution of the Lane–Emden equation[6]. With the CODATA constants[7] it gives 1.46 solar masses for $\mu_e = 2$ (carbon and oxygen) and 0.93 for $\mu_e = 2.5$. These are calculated values for an ideal cold gas.

Key Takeaway
  • No two electrons can share all four quantum numbers $n, l, m_l, m_s$ (Pauli, 1925), so each shell holds $2n^2$ electrons.
  • Filling states in order fills the outer s and p subshells at He, Ne, Ar and Kr, which gives the periodic table its rows.
  • Squeezed electrons resist through degeneracy pressure, $P \propto n^{5/3}$; when they become relativistic it softens to $n^{4/3}$, which sets the white-dwarf mass limit.
