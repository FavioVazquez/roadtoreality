Newton's Universal Gravitation

The same force that drops an apple holds the Moon in orbit

1687 · Isaac Newton

The question

How fast must a cannonball be fired sideways from a high mountain to fall all the way around the Earth instead of landing?

Newton's great insight was that the force pulling an apple to the ground and the force keeping the Moon in orbit are the same force — gravity — just acting at different distances. This seems obvious now. In 1687 it was revolutionary: the heavens and the Earth obeyed identical laws.

In A Treatise of the System of the World (written about 1685, printed in 1728) he illustrated it with a thought experiment: imagine a cannon on top of a very tall mountain. Fire a cannonball horizontally. It falls and lands nearby. Fire faster — it lands farther. Fire fast enough, and the Earth curves away as fast as the ball falls. It never lands. It orbits.

Drag the speed slider below. Watch the cannonball's path change from a short arc to a closed orbit.

From this 300 km mountain the circular-orbit speed is 7.7 km/s (7.9 at sea level); anything above 7.6 km/s clears the surface. Try values above and below.

What to try

  1. Press ▶ Fire! at the default Launch speed of 5.0 km/s: the red path curves down and the banner reads “Landed back on Earth. Above 7.6 km/s it clears the surface.”
  2. Set Launch speed to 7.6 km/s, then 7.7 km/s: at 7.6 the ball still comes down, much farther round; at 7.7 it goes all the way round on a green path and the banner reads “Orbiting Earth!” Raise Sim speed to 5× to see the laps sooner.
  3. Set Launch speed to 8.5 km/s: the orbit becomes a stretched ellipse whose far point, opposite the cannon, is about 1.6 Earth radii from the centre.
  4. Set Launch speed to 11.0 km/s, above the 10.9 km/s escape speed in the panel, and Sim speed to 5×: the yellow path heads away, and about half a minute later, once the ball is 20 Earth radii out, the banner reads “Escaped Earth gravity!”

The Inverse Square Law

In modern notation Newton's law is F = GMm/r²; Newton himself worked with proportions and never wrote G, which was first measured in 1798 by Cavendish. Double the distance, the force drops to a quarter. This precise mathematical relationship explained all of Kepler's empirical laws from a single principle.

The "universal" in universal gravitation was the radical claim: the same formula applies everywhere in the universe, at all scales, between all masses. The Moon, Jupiter, distant stars — all governed by the same equation. This was a profound unification of earthly and celestial physics.

Escape velocity — the speed needed to permanently leave Earth's gravity — is about 11.2 km/s. Low-orbit speed is lower, about 7.9 km/s at the surface and 7.7 km/s from the mountain, because you don't need to escape, just balance falling with horizontal speed. Try both in the simulation.

What the Simulation Leaves Out

The ball feels only the inverse-square pull of a smooth Earth that does not rotate (GM = 398,600 km³/s², radius 6,371 km), with no air drag, no Moon and no Sun. It is always fired horizontally from 300 km up. A ball counts as landed when it comes within 5 km of the surface and as escaped once it passes 20 Earth radii, about 127,000 km. Time runs at 200 simulated seconds per real second at 1×, and an orbit stops after three laps.

Key Takeaway
  • Orbiting IS falling — just with enough sideways speed that the Earth curves away as fast as you fall.
  • F = GMm/r²: gravity follows an inverse square law — double the distance, quarter the force.
  • Newton unified terrestrial and celestial mechanics: the same law governs apples and planets.

$$F = G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$$
